Paper of the day · arXiv 2018

nnU-Net

Self-adapting Framework for U-Net-Based Medical Image Segmentation

Fabian Isensee, Jens Petersen, Andre Klein et al. · 2018 · arXiv:1809.10486

Instead of inventing a new network for outlining organs and tumours in 3D scans, this paper takes the plain 2015 U-Net and writes down fixed rules that configure everything around it (patch size, depth, normalisation, loss, augmentation, ensembling) from the dataset's own statistics. With no hand-tuning, it topped a ten-dataset medical segmentation challenge.

I · In 60 Seconds

Problem10 datasets, 0 tweaks

Segmentation means labelling every voxel (3D pixel) of a scan: liver, tumour, or background. The standard network for it is the U-Net, which shrinks the image step by step to understand it, then grows it back to full size to draw precise outlines. Every year, papers propose new U-Net variants, each tested on one or two datasets.

The Medical Segmentation Decathlon asks for one method that works, fully automatically, on ten very different tasks: brain tumours in MRI (magnetic resonance imaging), livers in CT (X-ray computed tomography), a tiny brain structure called the hippocampus, the prostate, and more. Scans range from 36×50×35 voxels (hippocampus) to 482×512×512 (liver).

Idea"no-new-net"

The authors bet that the architecture is not the bottleneck; the choices around it are. nnU-Net keeps three plain U-Nets (one working on 2D slices, one on 3D blocks, and a two-stage 3D "cascade" for huge scans). It then derives every other setting by rule from the data: patch size and number of downsamplings from the median image shape, resampling from the median voxel spacing, intensity normalisation from the scan type. Each model is trained five times on different data splits, and the best model or pair of models is picked automatically.

Result#1 on 7 tasks

Scores are Dice overlaps between predicted and true regions (100 = perfect, defined below). On the seven public tasks' hidden test sets, nnU-Net had the best leaderboard score for every structure except one brain-tumour class: for example, liver 95.24, heart 92.77, hippocampus 90.37 and 88.95 (two sub-regions), pancreas 79.53 and pancreas tumour 52.27. Test scores matched the cross-validation scores, so it hadn't overfitted the development data.

II · The Linear Algebra

MachinerySparse operators, a projector, a ratio

This is an engineering paper with no theorem. But each of its rules is a statement about a specific linear operator, and the one real equation, the Dice loss, is an inner-product ratio whose geometry explains why it is used. Four objects carry the paper.

1 · The network is sparse Toeplitz operators and their transposes

Flatten a 3D image with \(n\) voxels into \(x\in\mathbb R^{n}\). A 3×3×3 convolution is a linear map \(C\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}\) that replaces each voxel by a fixed weighted sum of its 27 neighbours. So \(C\) is block-Toeplitz (constant along diagonals, because the same kernel is used everywhere) and extremely sparse: 27 nonzeros per row out of \(n\approx 10^6\). The U-Net's encoder (the shrinking half) applies two such convolutions, each followed by a pointwise nonlinearity, then halves the resolution along chosen axes. The decoder (the growing half) uses transposed convolutions: literally \(C_s^{\top}\), where \(C_s\) is a strided convolution (one that keeps only every second output). If \(C_s\) maps fine to coarse, \(C_s^{\top}\) maps coarse back to fine. It is the adjoint, \(\langle C_s x, y\rangle = \langle x, C_s^{\top} y\rangle\), not an inverse. Skip connections copy each encoder level's output across the U and stack it next to the decoder's upsampled features, so fine detail lost in downsampling is available again.

nnU-Net's contribution here is the shape rule. Take an axis whose patch length is \(s\) voxels. That axis is halved while it is still at least 8 voxels long, up to a cap \(p_{\max}\) (5 halvings in 3D, 6 in 2D):

$$p(s)=\min\!\big(p_{\max},\ \lfloor\log_2(s/8)\rfloor+1\big).$$

Prostate MRI has thick slices: the patch is 20×192×192, giving \(p=(2,5,5)\). The deepest level is 5×6×6, roughly cubic in physical space, so each deep kernel sees comparable physical extent in every direction. The network depth is different per axis, chosen by the data.

2 · Normalisation is an orthogonal projector

For a patch with values \(x\in\mathbb R^n\), let \(\mathbf 1\) be the all-ones vector and

$$H=I-\tfrac1n\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^{\top},\qquad z=\frac{Hx}{\|Hx\|/\sqrt n}.$$

\(H\) is the centering matrix: symmetric, idempotent (\(H^2=H\)), eigenvalues \(1\) (multiplicity \(n-1\)) and \(0\) (on \(\mathbf 1\)). It is the orthogonal projector onto \(\mathbf 1^{\perp}\), the vectors with mean zero. Dividing by \(\|Hx\|/\sqrt n\) (the standard deviation) puts \(z\) on the sphere of radius \(\sqrt n\) inside that hyperplane. This is a z-score. nnU-Net applies it to each MRI scan separately, and the network applies the same map to every feature channel of every sample (instance normalisation, used here instead of normalising across a batch).

The point: \(z\) is invariant under \(x\mapsto ax+b\mathbf 1\) for \(a>0\), since \(H\mathbf 1=0\) and the scale cancels. MRI intensities are in arbitrary, scanner-dependent units, so this invariance is wanted. CT intensities are physical (Hounsfield units: water 0, air −1000), so throwing away \(a\) and \(b\) per scan would discard real information. For CT, nnU-Net instead clips to the 0.5th–99.5th percentile of foreground intensities across the whole training set and uses one global mean and standard deviation.

Tiny example

\(x=(1,2,6)\), mean 3. \(Hx=(-2,-1,3)\), \(\|Hx\|^2=14\), standard deviation \(\sqrt{14/3}\approx2.16\), so \(z\approx(-0.93,-0.46,1.39)\).

Now take a brighter, higher-contrast scan of the same thing, \(x'=2x+5\mathbf 1=(7,9,17)\). Then \(Hx'=(-4,-2,6)=2Hx\), the standard deviation doubles too, and \(z'=z\). The two scans become identical inputs.

3 · The Dice loss: an inner product over two \(\ell_1\) norms

The network outputs, for each of \(|I|\) voxels and \(|K|\) classes, a probability. Stack them into \(u\in[0,1]^{I\times K}\); each row is a probability vector (the softmax output, rows sum to 1). The ground truth is \(v\in\{0,1\}^{I\times K}\), one-hot: each row is a standard basis vector \(e_k\) marking the true class. Write \(u^k, v^k\) for column \(k\). The paper's loss is

$$\mathcal L_{\text{dc}}=-\frac{2}{|K|}\sum_{k\in K}\frac{\sum_i u^k_i v^k_i}{\sum_i u^k_i+\sum_i v^k_i} =-\frac1{|K|}\sum_k\frac{2\langle u^k,v^k\rangle}{\|u^k\|_1+\|v^k\|_1},$$

added to the ordinary cross-entropy (the average over voxels of \(-\log\) of the probability given to the true class): \(\mathcal L_{\text{total}}=\mathcal L_{\text{dc}}+\mathcal L_{\text{CE}}\). When \(u\) is also 0/1, each fraction is the Dice coefficient of two sets \(A,B\) of voxels, \(2|A\cap B|/(|A|+|B|)\). That is the harmonic mean of precision (\(|A\cap B|/|A|\)) and recall (\(|A\cap B|/|B|\)), the same thing as the F1 score.

Why a ratio: it is blind to how much background there is

Pad the image with \(m\) more background voxels the network correctly ignores (\(u^k_i=v^k_i=0\)). Numerator and denominator of the class-\(k\) Dice term don't change. Cross-entropy is a mean over voxels, so a missed 6-voxel lesion costs on the order of \(6/(n+m)\) and vanishes as \(m\) grows. Dice is normalised by the size of the structure itself, so every class, whether a 95%-of-image liver or a 0.01% tumour, has equal weight \(1/|K|\).

With \(P=\langle u^k,v^k\rangle\) and \(S=\|u^k\|_1+\|v^k\|_1\), the gradient of one Dice term \(D=2P/S\) is

$$\frac{\partial D}{\partial u^k_i}=\frac{2\,(v^k_i\,S-P)}{S^2}.$$

It takes only two values: a positive push on every true-foreground voxel, and a uniform negative pull on every predicted-but-wrong voxel. The step size is set by the structure's size \(S\), not by the image size.

Tiny example

Three voxels, one class: \(u=(0.8,\,0.6,\,0.1)\), truth \(v=(1,1,0)\). \(P=1.4\), \(S=1.5+2=3.5\), so \(D=2.8/3.5=0.80\).

Gradient: on voxels 1, 2 it is \(2(3.5-1.4)/3.5^2\approx+0.34\); on voxel 3 it is \(2(0-1.4)/12.25\approx-0.23\). Append 1,000 more background voxels with \(u=0\): \(P, S, D\) and these gradients are unchanged.

4 · Resampling and the cascade are linear maps chosen by the data

Scanners record voxels of different physical sizes (spacing, in mm per voxel). A convolution kernel only means the same thing across patients if spacing is equal, so every scan is resampled to its dataset's median spacing. For images this uses cubic-spline interpolation, a linear operator. For label maps it uses nearest-neighbour, because labels are categories: averaging "liver = 1" and "tumour = 2" into 1.5 is meaningless. (Resampling the one-hot \(v\) linearly would be fine; resampling class indices is not.)

If the median resampled scan has more than 4× the voxels of one 3D training patch, nnU-Net adds the cascade. Stage 1 is a 3D U-Net run on a copy downsampled by factors of 2 (thin axes first, until spacing is roughly uniform), so it sees a whole organ in context. Its predicted segmentation is upsampled and appended as extra one-hot input channels for stage 2, a full-resolution 3D U-Net that refines the outline. This triggered on heart, liver, lung and pancreas.

IV · Cool Things

FindingsFrom the paper

Mirror averaging is a group projection\((\mathbb Z_2)^3\)

At test time each patch is also run flipped along every valid axis. The flips are permutation matrices \(g\) with \(g=g^{-1}=g^{\top}\), and in 3D they form a group of \(2^3=8\) elements. Averaging the network \(f\) over them,

$$\bar f(x)=\frac18\sum_{g}g\,f(gx),$$

gives an exactly flip-equivariant predictor: \(\bar f(hx)=h\bar f(x)\) for every flip \(h\), by reindexing \(g\mapsto gh\). The map \(f\mapsto\bar f\) is a projection onto equivariant functions, the same averaging trick as symmetrising a tensor or the Reynolds operator in invariant theory.

Where "up to 64 predictions" comes from\(2^3\times 2^3\)

Patches overlap by half a patch along each axis, so a voxel in the middle of a scan lies in \(2\) patches per axis, \(2^3=8\) in total. Each is evaluated under 8 flips: \(8\times8=64\) predictions per voxel, averaged with weights that favour patch centres, where the network sees the most context. Then the five cross-validation networks are averaged again. Convex combinations of probability vectors are still probability vectors, so all this averaging stays on the simplex.

Generalisation, honestly measured1 submission

The leaderboard allowed repeated test submissions; the authors submitted once. Cross-validation predicted the test score well: liver 94.3 → 95.2, heart 92.7 → 92.8, pancreas 79.3 → 79.5. The only big drop was brain tumour (e.g. 80.7 → 67.7 on one class), a dataset known for a shift between training and test scans.

Topology as postprocessing\(\beta_0=1\)

If a class is a single connected blob in every training label (a liver has one component), that is taken as a property of the dataset, and at test time all but the largest predicted component is deleted. This is a learned constraint on the zeroth Betti number (component count), enforced combinatorially rather than inside the network.

V · If You Remember One Thing

The one thing to remember

A plain U-Net, with its patch shape, depth, intensity normalisation and loss derived by rule from each dataset's statistics, beat specialised architectures across seven different medical tasks. The settings around a network mattered more than changes to the network itself.
