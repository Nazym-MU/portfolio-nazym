Mean Teacher

Mean teachers are better role models: Weight-averaged consistency targets improve semi-supervised deep learning results

Antti Tarvainen & Harri Valpola · 2017 · arXiv:1703.01780

To learn from images that mostly have no labels, the paper trains a network to agree with a second copy of itself whose weights are a running average of its own past weights. That averaged copy makes better targets than the network itself, at the cost of one extra forward pass.

I · In 60 Seconds

Problem250 labels, 73k images

Semi-supervised learning: you have a few labelled images and many unlabelled ones. A common trick is consistency: a good classifier should give the same answer when its input is slightly perturbed (shifted a pixel, noise added) or when random internal units are switched off (dropout). So for an unlabelled image, run the network twice with different random noise and penalise disagreement. One run serves as the teacher that produces a target. The other is the student that gets trained toward it.

If the teacher is just the student itself (the earlier “Π model”), its targets are as wrong as the student, and the student learns to agree with its own mistakes. Temporal Ensembling improved targets by averaging each image's predictions over training, but each image's average is only refreshed once per pass through the dataset (an epoch). On big or streaming data that is far too slow.

Idea\(\theta' \leftarrow \alpha\theta' + (1-\alpha)\theta\)

Average the weights, not the predictions. The teacher is the same network architecture whose weights \(\theta'\) are an exponential moving average of the student's weights \(\theta\), updated after every training step with \(\alpha = 0.999\): the teacher keeps 99.9% of its old weights and takes 0.1% from the student. The teacher is never trained by gradient descent. It is a time-average of the student that is refreshed every step.

Result27.8% → 4.35% error

On SVHN (photos of house-number digits, 73,257 training images) with only 250 labels, training on labels alone gives 27.77% test error. The Π model gives 9.69%, and Mean Teacher gives 4.35%. That beats Temporal Ensembling trained with four times as many labels (4.42% at 1000).

On CIFAR-10 (tiny 32×32 photos of 10 object classes) with 4000 of 50,000 labels and a deeper residual network, error falls from the previous best of 10.55% to 6.28%. On ImageNet (1.2M photos, 1000 classes) with 10% of labels, it falls from 35.24% to 9.11%.

II · The Linear Algebra

MachineryA linear filter on weight space

The network is a classifier with \(N\) classes (\(N=10\) here). Given an image \(x\), it outputs a probability vector, so the output lives on the simplex \(\{p\in\mathbb R^N : p_i\ge 0,\ \textstyle\sum_i p_i=1\}\).

The objects

\(\theta\in\mathbb R^P\)student, trained

Every weight of the network, flattened into one long vector of length \(P\). Gradient descent updates it.

\(\theta'\in\mathbb R^P\)teacher, averaged

Same architecture, same shape, different numbers. It is a fixed linear function of the student's history (below) and receives no gradient.

\(\eta,\ \eta'\)independent noise

Each side gets its own random draw of perturbations: a random shift of up to 2 pixels, Gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.15 added to the input, and dropout. Dropout multiplies a layer's activation vector by a random diagonal 0/1 matrix \(D\), with each diagonal entry 0 with probability ½.

\(f(x,\theta,\eta)\in\mathbb R^N\)softmax output

The predicted class probabilities: \(p=\operatorname{softmax}(z)\), where \(z\in\mathbb R^N\) are the network's final scores (logits) and \(p_i = e^{z_i}/\sum_j e^{z_j}\).

Let \(C(\theta)\) be the usual cross-entropy on the labelled images and \(w(t)\) a weight that ramps up from 0 over the first 40,000 steps. The student minimises

$$C(\theta) + w(t)\,J(\theta),\qquad J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{x,\eta,\eta'}\Big[\big\|f(x,\theta',\eta')-f(x,\theta,\eta)\big\|^2\Big],$$

where \(x\) runs over all images, labelled or not, and \(\theta'\) is held constant when differentiating. On SVHN each minibatch holds 1 labelled image and 99 unlabelled ones.

Because the two noise draws are independent, \(J\) splits by the usual bias–variance identity. Write \(\bar a=\mathbb E_{\eta'}f(x,\theta',\eta')\) and \(\bar b=\mathbb E_{\eta}f(x,\theta,\eta)\) for each side's mean prediction:

$$J=\mathbb E_x\Big[\ \|\bar a-\bar b\|^2\ +\ \operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Cov}_{\eta}\,f(x,\theta,\eta)\ +\ \operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Cov}_{\eta'}\,f(x,\theta',\eta')\ \Big].$$

The last term has zero gradient with respect to \(\theta\). So the student is pushed toward two things: its average prediction should match the teacher's average, and its prediction should not depend on the noise (small covariance trace). The second is the invariance; the first is where the quality of the teacher matters.

The teacher is a geometric convolution of the student's path

With \(\theta_s\) the student after step \(s\), unrolling \(\theta'_t=\alpha\theta'_{t-1}+(1-\alpha)\theta_t\) gives

$$\theta'_t=\alpha^t\theta'_0+(1-\alpha)\sum_{s=1}^{t}\alpha^{\,t-s}\,\theta_s .$$

This is a convex combination of past iterates with geometrically decaying weights, a one-pole low-pass filter applied coordinatewise in \(\mathbb R^P\). The weights have mean age \(\sum_k k(1-\alpha)\alpha^k=\alpha/(1-\alpha)\) steps. If the student drifts at constant velocity \(v\), the teacher trails it by exactly \(v\cdot\alpha/(1-\alpha)\): 99 steps behind at \(\alpha=0.99\), 999 at \(\alpha=0.999\). That lag is why the paper uses \(\alpha=0.99\) early in training, when the student improves fast, and 0.999 afterwards.

Why an averaged network is better: the Hessian's spectrum

The paper justifies this step only by citing Polyak & Juditsky (1992), who showed that averaging the iterates of stochastic gradient descent speeds up convergence. Here is the standard argument behind that citation, worked out for the EMA. Near a minimum \(\theta^*\), model the loss as quadratic with Hessian \(H\), which is symmetric positive semidefinite. Let \(e_t=\theta_t-\theta^*\) be the error, \(\eta_{\rm lr}\) the learning rate, and \(\xi_t\) zero-mean gradient noise from sampling minibatches. Stochastic gradient descent is then the linear recursion

$$e_{t+1}=(I-\eta_{\rm lr}H)\,e_t-\eta_{\rm lr}\,\xi_t .$$

By the spectral theorem, \(H=Q\Lambda Q^\top\) with \(Q\) orthogonal. In the eigenbasis, the recursion decouples into independent scalar processes, one per eigenvalue \(\lambda_i\), each with its own decay factor \(\rho_i=1-\eta_{\rm lr}\lambda_i\). Assume the noise is isotropic, \(\operatorname{Cov}\xi=\sigma^2 I\). Then each coordinate settles to a stationary variance \(\eta_{\rm lr}^2\sigma^2/(1-\rho_i^2)\), and its contribution to the excess loss, \(\tfrac12\lambda_i\operatorname{Var}\), is about \(\eta_{\rm lr}\sigma^2/4\) in every direction. So the noise costs roughly the same loss in every eigendirection.

Pass coordinate \(i\) through the EMA. Summing the double geometric series \(\sum_{j,k}\alpha^{j+k}\rho^{|j-k|}\) gives the stationary variance ratio

$$\frac{\operatorname{Var}(\text{teacher}_i)}{\operatorname{Var}(\text{student}_i)}=\frac{(1-\alpha)(1+\alpha\rho_i)}{(1+\alpha)(1-\alpha\rho_i)} .$$

For a stiff direction (large \(\lambda_i\), \(\rho_i\) well below 1) the ratio is close to \((1-\alpha)/(1+\alpha)\), a huge cut. For a flat direction (\(\rho_i\to1\)) it tends to 1, because the student's error there is already slow and the filter cannot separate it from signal. With \(\alpha=0.999\): \(\rho=0.9\) gives about 0.009, while \(\rho=0.999\) gives 0.5.

Tiny example

\(H=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\), with eigenvalues 3 and 1 on eigenvectors \((1,1)/\sqrt2\) and \((1,-1)/\sqrt2\). Take \(\eta_{\rm lr}=0.1\). Then \(I-\eta_{\rm lr}H=\begin{pmatrix}0.8&-0.1\\-0.1&0.8\end{pmatrix}\), with the same eigenvectors and eigenvalues \(\rho=0.7\) and \(0.9\).

With \(\alpha=0.9\), the stiff direction's ratio is \(\frac{0.1\cdot1.63}{1.9\cdot0.37}\approx0.23\) and the flat direction's is \(\frac{0.1\cdot1.81}{1.9\cdot0.19}\approx0.50\). The teacher has about a quarter of the student's jitter along \((1,1)\) and half along \((1,-1)\). The averaging works best where the loss is most sensitive.

What the consistency gradient does: the softmax Jacobian

Let \(p\) be the student's output and \(q\) the teacher's, and differentiate with respect to the student's logits \(z\). The Jacobian of softmax is

$$\frac{\partial p}{\partial z}=\operatorname{diag}(p)-pp^\top=:S_p .$$

\(S_p\) is the covariance matrix of a one-hot vector \(e_Y\) with the class \(Y\) drawn from \(p\). So it is symmetric and PSD, with \(S_p\mathbf 1=0\) (adding a constant to all logits changes nothing), and \(S_p\preceq\operatorname{diag}(p)\preceq(\max_i p_i)I\). The two candidate costs then give

$$\nabla_z\,\tfrac12\|p-q\|^2=S_p\,(p-q),\qquad \nabla_z\,D_{\rm KL}(q\,\|\,p)=p-q .$$

Here \(D_{\rm KL}\) is the Kullback–Leibler divergence, the natural mismatch measure between distributions. Squared error is the KL gradient passed through the PSD filter \(S_p\). When the student is confident (\(p\) close to one-hot), \(S_p\to0\), so a teacher that disagrees can barely move it.

Tiny example

\(N=3\), student \(p=(0.7,0.2,0.1)\), teacher \(q=(0.5,0.3,0.2)\), so \(p-q=(0.2,-0.1,-0.1)\).

\(S_p=\begin{pmatrix}0.21&-0.14&-0.07\\-0.14&0.16&-0.02\\-0.07&-0.02&0.09\end{pmatrix}\). Its rows sum to 0, as they must.

The squared-error gradient is \(S_p(p-q)=(0.063,\,-0.042,\,-0.021)\). The KL gradient is \((0.2,-0.1,-0.1)\). Both lower the top class, but squared error does so about 3× more gently.

The appendix makes the link exact. To second order around \(p=q\), \(D_{\rm KL}(q\|p)\approx\tfrac12\sum_i (p_i-q_i)^2/q_i\): KL is squared distance in the metric \(\operatorname{diag}(1/q)\) (the Fisher information metric). The authors blend both distributions toward uniform, \(p_\tau=\tau p+(1-\tau)/N\), which turns the metric into \(\approx N\,I\). Their rescaled cost \(C_\tau(p,q)=\tfrac{2}{N^2\tau^2}D_{\rm KL}(p_\tau\|q_\tau)\) then runs from plain squared error (\(\tau\to0\)) to KL (\(\tau=1\)). Squared error, the Euclidean end, wins on 250-label SVHN.

III · Try It: Average Away the Noise

InteractiveNoisy SGD vs. its EMA

A two-weight “network” with a quadratic loss whose Hessian has a stiff eigenvalue \(\lambda=4\) and a flat one \(\lambda=4/\kappa\). Here \(\kappa\) is the condition number, the ratio of the largest curvature to the smallest. The student runs 2000 steps of noisy gradient descent (learning rate 0.1) from a far-off start. The teacher is its EMA with decay \(\alpha\), started at the same point. Left: the paths over the loss's contour ellipses. Right: excess loss over time, on a log scale.

Try: set \(\alpha=0\), then slide up through 0.9 and 0.99 to 0.999. Notice: the thick teacher line smooths more and more, and its loss floor drops well below the student's jagged one. At 0.999 it lags so far behind that it is still high at the end, which is the reason for the paper's 0.99-then-0.999 schedule. Then raise \(\kappa\) and compare the two variance rows: the cut is large in the stiff direction and much smaller in the flat one, as the formula above predicts.

IV · Cool Things

FindingsFrom the paper's tables

Averaging weights of a nonlinear net shouldn't work, but does\(f(\bar\theta)\approx\overline{f(\theta)}\)

For a nonlinear network, the averaged weights give a different function from the average of the networks' outputs. Here the two agree to first order: consecutive iterates lie in one small region where \(f\) is nearly linear in \(\theta\), so \(f(x,\sum_s c_s\theta_s)\approx\sum_s c_s f(x,\theta_s)\). The teacher is then a free ensemble over about 1000 recent students. It also improves every intermediate layer, not just the output.

A virtuous loopFig. 3

The teacher improves the student through the consistency cost, and the student improves the teacher through the average. With 500 labels on SVHN, the Π model stops improving while Mean Teacher keeps going. With all labels present, the two behave identically, so the gain comes entirely from the unlabelled data.

More unlabelled data keeps paying4.18% → 2.46%

On SVHN, the labels stay fixed at 500 while the unlabelled pool grows from 73k to 573k images, and error drops from 4.18% to 2.46%. Temporal Ensembling could not do this, because its targets refresh once per epoch and an epoch over 573k images is slow. Keeping each target fresh at a fixed time span would take \(O(n^2)\) evaluations per epoch for \(n\) images.

Label propagation with a learned metricgraphs

Classic label propagation spreads labels along a similarity graph with a fixed distance on inputs. Consistency training does the same, except the distance is the one the network's own features induce. That distance improves as the features improve, so the graph is effectively re-learned during training.

V · If You Remember One Thing

The one thing to remember

An exponential moving average of a network's own weights is a low-pass filter that removes most of SGD's jitter in the stiff directions of the loss. That gives a better network for free, and training the student to agree with it on unlabelled images lets 250 labels do the work of thousands.
