Combinatorial Surfaces

How to say “surface” using nothing but sets of vertices

no coordinates, no curvature — just who touches whom

A triangle mesh is two things stacked together: geometry (where each vertex sits in space) and connectivity (which vertices are joined into edges and triangles). This page is only about the second.

Throw the coordinates away and a surface becomes a finite list of finite sets. The payoff: “is this actually a surface?” turns into a check you can run vertex by vertex, exactly, with no tolerances.

In 60 Seconds

  • A mesh as lists. Number the vertices 1, 2, 3, … and record each triangle as the set of its three numbers, like {3, 5, 2}. Edges are pairs. Positions are never stored.
  • The question. Triangles can be glued in ways no real surface allows: three triangles sharing one edge, or two cones touching only at their tips. A genuine surface looks like a small flat disk wherever you zoom in.
  • The test. Pick a vertex and look at the triangles around it. Their far edges, the ones that don’t touch the vertex, form a ring called the link. The mesh is a surface exactly when every vertex’s link is one closed loop (or one open path, for vertices on the outer rim).
  • How code stores it. Split every edge into two arrows pointing opposite ways, one for each triangle beside it (“halfedges”). Two pointer rules, twin (flip to the opposite arrow) and next (move to the next arrow around the same triangle), recover every face, edge and vertex. Alternating them walks you around a vertex, and the surface test becomes: does the walk come back?

I · The Definitions

Sets of verticesNo coordinates

Simplexthe building block

A k-simplex is the simplest k-dimensional piece: a point (k = 0), a segment (1), a triangle (2), a tetrahedron (3). It needs k + 1 vertices, since the first fixes a location and each further one adds a direction.

With connectivity all we keep, a simplex is its vertex set: {3, 5, 2} is a triangle, {1, 2} an edge. Its faces are its nonempty subsets (a triangle’s three edges, three corners, and itself). A proper face is any face other than the whole simplex.

Simplicial complexclosed under subsets

A simplicial complex, written 𝒲, is a collection of simplices with one rule: if a simplex (call it σ) is in 𝒲, so is every face of σ.

The purpose is bookkeeping. Declaring a triangle implicitly declares its edges and corners; the rule makes them explicitly present, so any operation can reach down to them. A subcomplex is a subset that obeys the rule on its own: {{1, 2}} is not one, but {{1, 2}, {1}, {2}} is.

Pureno stray pieces

A complex is pure of dimension 2 when every edge and every vertex lies in at least one triangle. It rules out debris: an edge dangling off the mesh, or a lone vertex floating beside it. A surface is triangles and nothing else.

Star, closure, linkwhat is near a vertex

To ask whether a mesh looks like a disk near a vertex i, we need a precise meaning for “near i”. Three operations supply it.

The star, written St(i), is every simplex that contains i: the vertex, its edges, its triangles. It is an open fan, and usually not a complex: it holds the triangle {i, j, k} but not its far edge {j, k}, which doesn’t contain i.

The closure of a collection, written Cl, is the smallest complex containing it: add every missing face. Cl(St(i)) is the closed fan, far edges included.

The link, written Lk(i), is the closed fan minus everything touching i:

Lk(i) = Cl(St(i)) \ St(i)

Example: if six triangles surround i, the closed fan has 6 triangles, 12 edges and 7 vertices. Deleting what touches i removes i, its 6 spokes and the 6 triangles, leaving 6 rim edges on 6 rim vertices: a hexagon. That ring is what the surface test inspects.

Boundaryface of exactly one

Boundary is defined by counting. In a pure patch of triangles, an interior edge is a side of two triangles; a rim edge is a side of only one. So the boundary is: every simplex that is a proper face of exactly one triangle, then closed up so the rim edges bring their endpoints. The interior is everything else. The definition just notices where a second triangle failed to show up.

II · What Makes a Surface

The conditionEvery link is a loop

The link test

A simplicial surface is a pure 2-dimensional complex in which the link of every vertex is a single closed loop of edges. A complex passing this test is called manifold: near every point it looks like a flat disk.

Why a loop is the right demand: walk around i from triangle to triangle through shared edges. A single loop means the walk meets each triangle once and returns to the start, so the fan closes into a disk.

If the mesh may have an outer rim, a rim vertex’s link is a single open path instead, and its fan is a half-disk. Reading the definition word by word gives every way it can fail:

  • Single. Two separate loops (or paths) in one link mean two fans pinched at one vertex, like an hourglass. Delete that vertex and the mesh falls apart, which no point of a real surface does.
  • Loop. An open path means the fan never closes: a rim vertex. Fine for a surface with boundary, not for a closed one.
  • Of edges, no branching. If three triangles share the edge {i, j}, each contributes a link edge at j, so three link edges meet there. That branch point is a nonmanifold edge: a flat disk has only two sides to any line.

n-manifoldlink = sphere

The test generalizes by one substitution. Write 𝕊n for the n-dimensional sphere, the unit-length points of ℝn+1: 𝕊1 is a circle, 𝕊2 an ordinary sphere, 𝕊0 the two points ±1.

A simplicial n-manifold is a pure n-dimensional complex whose every vertex link is a triangulated (n−1)-sphere. For n = 2 that is a loop: the surface case. For n = 3, a tetrahedral mesh where each vertex is wrapped in a triangulated sphere. For n = 1, every vertex has exactly two neighbors, so a finite 1-manifold is a union of closed loops. Same idea each time: the rim of a small n-dimensional neighborhood is an (n−1)-sphere.

III · The Halfedge Mesh

Data structureOne set, two maps

The definitions answer “which triangles touch i?” by searching a list. A halfedge mesh answers by following pointers.

Split each edge {i, j} into two arrows: ij (from i to j) and ji. These are halfedges, and H is the set of all of them. Orient every triangle the same way round; then each triangle owns the three arrows circling it, and an edge’s two arrows belong to the triangles on its two sides. Without a rim, every edge gives exactly two, so H has twice as many elements as there are edges.

Only two maps from H to itself are stored. Twin, written η (eta), flips an arrow, crossing into the neighboring triangle; twice is the identity. Next, written ρ (rho), moves to the following arrow around the same triangle; three steps bring you home.

η(ij) = ji      ρ(ij) = jk  (for the triangle ijk)

Faces, edges, vertices as orbits

Nothing else is stored. Start at one halfedge and apply a map over and over until you return; the halfedges visited form its orbit. Every mesh element is one:

Face = orbit of next: ij → jk → ki → ij, length 3.

Edge = orbit of twin: ij → ji → ij, length 2.

Vertex = orbit of “twin, then next”, written ρ ∘ η. Start at ij, leaving i. Its twin ji points back into i, and next moves on to the arrow leaving i in the neighboring triangle. Each step turns one triangle around i.

So the orbit’s length is the number of triangles at i, and the orbit closing up after meeting each of them once is the single-loop link test. Checking that a mesh is manifold becomes checking that pointers come back around.

If You Remember One Thing

A surface is one local condition: the ring of edges opposite each vertex is a single loop. Code checks it by walking twin-then-next until it comes back; coordinates come afterwards.
