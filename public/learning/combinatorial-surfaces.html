Combinatorial Surfaces

How to say “surface” using nothing but sets of vertices

no coordinates, no curvature — just who touches whom

A triangle mesh has two halves that come apart cleanly. There is the geometry: where each vertex sits in space, and everything that depends on position, such as length, angle, area, curvature. And there is the connectivity: which vertices form an edge, which edges bound a triangle, what is glued to what. This page is entirely about the second half.

Stripping the coordinates away is not an act of poverty. It buys precision. Once a surface is a purely combinatorial object, a finite family of finite sets, questions that are slippery in the smooth world become checkable by counting. Is this thing actually a surface? Does it have a boundary? Can I walk around a vertex and get back where I started? Each becomes a condition you can verify on a laptop, exactly, with no epsilons.

The path runs in one direction. Build a piece (the simplex), collect pieces under a closure rule (the complex), look at what surrounds a piece (star, closure, link), and then impose one local condition on those surroundings that promotes a bag of triangles into a surface. The last section swaps the whole picture for a set of arrows and two permutations, which is how a computer actually stores it.

I · The Simplex

Building blockk-simplex

Start with the smallest possible piece of a shape. A k-simplex is the k-dimensional shape formed by the convex hull of k + 1 vertices. The convex hull is the smallest convex set containing those points, which you can picture as the shape a rubber sheet takes when stretched around them.

The count is the part worth internalizing. It takes 1 point to pin down a 0-dimensional thing, 2 to span a line, 3 to span a triangle, 4 to span a tetrahedron. Dimension is always one less than the number of vertices, because the first vertex only fixes a location and each vertex after it contributes one new direction to move in.

Once connectivity is all that matters, a simplex needs no coordinates at all. It is just the set of its vertices. The 2-simplex {3, 5, 2} is a triangle on those three vertices. The 1-simplex {1, 2} is the edge joining vertex 1 to vertex 2. Written this way the object is called an abstract simplex, and it carries exactly the information we care about: which vertices are involved.

Every nonempty subset of a simplex is again a simplex, called a face. The triangle {3, 5, 2} has faces {3, 5}, {5, 2}, {3, 2} (its three edges), the three single vertices, and the triangle itself. A face that is not the whole simplex is a proper face. That word does real work in the boundary definition later.

Keep hold of

A k-simplex has k + 1 vertices. Abstractly it is its vertex set, and its faces are the nonempty subsets of that set.

II · Simplicial Complexes & Purity

Assembly ruleClosed under subsets

Now collect simplices. Not any collection will do. An abstract simplicial complex is a collection of abstract simplices that is closed under taking subsets: for every simplex σ in the complex K, every face σ′ ⊆ σ is also in K.

The condition says something modest and necessary. If you have declared that a triangle is part of your mesh, you have implicitly declared that its edges and its vertices are part of it too. There is no such thing as a triangle whose edges do not exist. Bookkeeping this way means every piece a shape is built from is present in the list, so any operation you define can reach downward without falling off the edge of the data.

SubcomplexK′ ⊆ K

A subcomplex is a subset of a complex that is itself a complex. So it must be closed under subsets in its own right. This is why a single edge, taken alone, is not a subcomplex of anything: the set {{1, 2}} leaves out the vertices {1} and {2}. Add them and you have a subcomplex. The lesson is that a valid piece of a mesh always drags its faces along with it.

Pure k-simplicial complexno strays

A complex is pure of degree k when every simplex in it is contained in some simplex of degree k (possibly itself). For k = 2 this says every vertex and every edge belongs to at least one triangle. A collection of triangles with a stray edge poking off the side, or a lone vertex floating in the middle of nowhere, fails the test. Purity is what rules out debris of lower dimension hanging off an otherwise two-dimensional object.

III · Star, Closure, Link

Local structureThree operators

To say anything about a surface locally, you need a way to ask what surrounds a given piece. Three operators do this. Each takes a subset S of a complex and returns another subset. Only the last two are guaranteed to return complexes.

Star  St(S)everything above

The star of S is the collection of all simplices in K that contain any simplex of S. For a single vertex i, St(i) is every simplex σ with i ∈ σ: the vertex itself, every edge incident on it, every triangle touching it. Think of it as the umbrella of things resting on top of S.

The star is usually not a complex. St(i) contains a triangle ijk but not the opposite edge jk, since jk does not contain i. It is open at the outer rim, which is exactly the property the next two operators are built to exploit.

Closure  Cl(S)smallest repair

The closure of S is the smallest subcomplex of K containing S, smallest meaning fewest elements. Concretely, take S and throw in every face of every one of its simplices, which is the least you can add to satisfy the subset-closure rule. Closure is the repair operation: hand it any ragged collection and it hands back a legitimate complex.

Link  Lk(S)the rim

The link is the two combined:

Lk(S) = Cl(St(S)) \ St(Cl(S))

Read it as a subtraction. Cl(St(S)) is the whole neighborhood of S patched up into a complex, everything touching S plus the faces needed to make that legal. St(Cl(S)) is everything that touches S or any of its faces. Removing the second from the first deletes every simplex still in contact with S and leaves only the outer rim: the part of the neighborhood that surrounds S without meeting it.

For a vertex i in a triangle mesh this cancellation is easy to see. Cl(St(i)) is the fan of triangles around i together with all their edges and vertices. St(Cl(i)) is everything containing i. What survives is the ring of edges opposite i, the boundary of the fan. That ring is the object the surface condition tests.

IV · Boundary & Interior

Counting testExactly one

The word boundary has an intuitive meaning and here it gets a definition that matches it by counting. For a pure k-subcomplex K′ ⊆ K, the boundary bd(K′) is the closure of the set of all simplices σ that are proper faces of exactly one simplex of K′.

The counting is the whole idea. Take a patch of triangles. An interior edge sits between two triangles, so it is a proper face of two of them. An edge along the rim has a triangle on one side and nothing on the other, so it is a proper face of exactly one. Collect those rim edges, take the closure to bring in their endpoints, and you have the boundary loop. The definition is doing nothing more exotic than noticing where the second triangle failed to show up.

The interior is then everything else:

int(K′) = K′ \ bd(K′)

Why the closure is in there

Without it you would collect a set of edges with no vertices, which is not a complex, exactly the single-edge problem from section II. Taking the closure makes the boundary a genuine subcomplex, so you can then apply star, link, or boundary to it again.

V · What Makes a Surface a Surface

The conditionEvery link is a loop

Here is the payoff. An abstract simplicial surface is a pure simplicial 2-complex in which the link of every vertex is a single loop of edges. Equivalently, the star of every vertex is a combinatorial disk made of triangles.

Pure and 2-dimensional means you have triangles and nothing but triangles, with no lower-dimensional debris. The link condition is what turns that pile of triangles into a surface. It is a purely local test, applied once per vertex, and it encodes the defining property of a surface: every point has a neighborhood that looks like a flat disk. A complex satisfying it is called manifold.

Think about why a loop is the right thing to demand. Walk around vertex i, crossing from one incident triangle to the next through their shared edges. If the link is a single closed loop, that walk visits every triangle around i exactly once and returns to where it began, without branching and without dead ends. That is precisely what it means for the fan around i to be a disk. Break the loop and the disk breaks with it.

Word by wordwhy each fails

Single. Two separate loops in one link mean two cones of triangles pinched together at a single shared vertex, like an hourglass. Each cone is disk-like on its own, but their union is not. Cut the vertex and the object falls into two pieces, which no point of a real surface does.

Loop, not a path. A link that is an open arc rather than a closed cycle means the fan around i does not close up. That vertex sits on a boundary, so its neighborhood is a half-disk. Legitimate for a surface with boundary, but it fails the closed-surface definition, which is why boundary gets its own treatment in section IV.

Of edges. The link has to be 1-dimensional. If three triangles share one edge, the link of a vertex on that edge contains a branch point where three arcs meet, so it is not a loop. This is the classic nonmanifold edge, and no arrangement of a real surface produces one, because a surface is locally flat and a flat disk has only two sides to an edge.

The one thing to remember

A surface is not defined by how it looks from far away. It is defined by one local condition checked at every vertex: the link is a single loop of edges. Get that right everywhere and the global thing is a surface, whatever shape it turns out to have.

VI · Spheres & n-Manifolds

GeneralizationLink = (n−1)-sphere

The surface condition generalizes, and the generalization explains why a loop was the right thing to ask for in the first place. First, the sphere. The n-sphere is

𝕊n := { x ∈ ℝn+1 : |x| = 1 }

the set of all points at unit distance from the origin in (n + 1)-dimensional space. Note the index: the sphere is n-dimensional but lives in one dimension more, because the constraint |x| = 1 removes one degree of freedom. A simplicial n-sphere is any triangulation of it, a complex glued together in that pattern.

The small casesn = 0, 1, 2

𝕊2 is the ordinary unit sphere in ℝ3. Triangulated, it is something like an icosahedron.

𝕊1 is the unit circle in the plane. Triangulated, it is a closed loop of edges. This is the link condition from section V, restated.

𝕊0 is {x ∈ ℝ : |x| = 1}, which is just the two points ±1. A pair of points, and it is a legitimate sphere: the pattern holds all the way down.

Now the general definition. A simplicial n-manifold is a pure simplicial n-complex in which the link of every vertex is a simplicial (n − 1)-sphere. One statement, and the dimension is a parameter.

n = 2  ·  surfacelink = 𝕊1

Every link is a simplicial 1-sphere, a closed loop of edges. This is exactly the surface definition, recovered as a special case.

n = 3  ·  tet meshlink = 𝕊2

A tetrahedral mesh in which every vertex is enclosed by a triangulation of the sphere. Stand at a vertex inside the mesh and the tetrahedra around you form a little ball, whose surface is that sphere.

n = 1  ·  curvelink = 𝕊0

Every vertex has a link that is a pair of points, meaning exactly two neighbors: one on each side. A vertex with one neighbor would be a loose end, and a vertex with three would be a junction, and both are barred. Force that at every vertex of a finite complex and the only thing you can build is a collection of closed loops. The low-dimensional case is where the definition is easiest to check by hand.

The pattern

Manifold means locally flat, and “locally flat in n dimensions” is encoded as “the boundary of my neighborhood is an (n − 1)-sphere.” A disk's rim is a circle; a ball's surface is a sphere; a segment's ends are two points. Same statement, three dimensions.

VII · The Halfedge Mesh

Data structureOne set, two maps

Everything so far is a definition. This section is how you store it. The simplicial view keeps a list of vertex sets and answers questions like “which triangles touch this vertex” by searching. The halfedge view replaces the search with pointer-following, and it does so by throwing away the sets entirely and keeping only arrows.

Halfedgean oriented edge

For every unoriented edge {i, j} between vertices i and j, keep two oriented edges, ij and ji. They are distinct: ij ≠ ji. These are the halfedges, and H is the set of all of them. On a surface without boundary every edge is shared by exactly two triangles and so yields exactly two halfedges, giving |H| = 2|E|.

Orientation is the point. An unoriented edge cannot tell you which side you are on. A halfedge can, so you can adopt the convention that ij belongs to the triangle on its left and ji to the triangle on its right. Each triangle then owns three halfedges pointing consistently around it.

Twin  ηη : H → H

The twin map sends a halfedge to the one with the same endpoints and the opposite direction:

η(ij) = ji

Twin is how you cross an edge into the neighboring triangle. Applying it twice returns you to where you started, so η ∘ η is the identity.

Next  ρρ : H → H

The next map advances to the following halfedge around the same triangle:

ρ(ij) = jk   for every triangle ijk ∈ K

Note that the target of ij is the source of jk, so next walks forward along the triangle without leaving it. Three applications inside a triangle bring you back to the start.

VIII · Everything Is an Orbit

The trickFaces, edges, vertices from η and ρ

An orbit is the sequence you get by applying a map to its own output over and over, starting from some element, until you cycle back to where you began. The elegance of the halfedge structure is that the mesh's faces, edges, and vertices are not stored anywhere. They are the orbits of η and ρ.

Facesorbits of ρ

Start at i1i2 and apply ρ repeatedly. Each step moves to the next halfedge around the same triangle, so the walk goes around the face and returns to i1i2. On a triangle mesh every such orbit has length three, and the set of halfedges it visits is the face.

Edgesorbits of η

Start at ij, apply η to get ji, apply it again to get back ij. Every orbit has length two, and the pair {ij, ji} is the unoriented edge. This is the same fact as |H| = 2|E|, read off the structure instead of counted.

Verticesorbits of ρ ∘ η

This is the interesting one. Compose the two maps, remembering that ∘ is function composition, so the right map goes first: twin, then next. Take a halfedge pointing away from vertex i. Its twin points back toward i. Applying next to that twin gives the following halfedge around the neighboring triangle, which points away from i again. So one application of ρ ∘ η rotates you by one triangle around i, and the orbit sweeps the entire fan and comes back.

That orbit is the vertex, and its length is the vertex's degree. This also gives you the surface condition for free: the fan closes up precisely when the orbit returns to its starting halfedge having visited each incident triangle once, which is the single-loop link from section V. Checking whether a mesh is manifold turns into checking whether some pointers come back around.

The one thing to remember

A combinatorial surface needs no coordinates. Give a computer a set of halfedges and two maps on it, twin and next, and faces, edges, vertices, and the manifold condition all fall out as orbits. Geometry is the layer you add on top afterwards, by assigning each vertex a position.

shape is what is left when you take the numbers away.
