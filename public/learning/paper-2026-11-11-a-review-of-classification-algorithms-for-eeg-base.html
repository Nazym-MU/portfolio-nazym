I · In 60 Seconds

Problemfew, noisy, drifting

A brain–computer interface (BCI) lets someone control a device by thought alone. The most common kind uses EEG (electroencephalography): a few to a few dozen electrodes on the scalp, each recording a voltage hundreds of times a second. The system cuts the recording into short trials, say the two seconds after a cue, and a classifier must decide what the user was doing in each, for example imagining moving the left hand or the right.

That is hard. The signal is weak and smeared by the skull, every user calibrates on only a few dozen trials, and the statistics drift between sessions, and even within one. A 2007 review by Lotte and colleagues covered the classifiers of its time; this paper updates it with a decade of new ones.

Ideafour new families

The review sorts the new methods into four families. Adaptive classifiers keep updating their parameters during use, with or without knowing the true labels. Matrix and tensor classifiers classify a whole matrix per trial, typically the channel-by-channel covariance, using the geometry of positive-definite matrices (Riemannian methods). Transfer learning reuses data from other sessions or other users. Deep learning trains neural networks on raw or lightly processed signals. It also revisits two older tools for small training sets: shrinkage LDA, a linear classifier with a regularised covariance, and random forests, which vote over many decision trees.

Resultgeometry beats depth (2018)

Adaptive classifiers generally beat static ones, even when they adapt without labels. Transfer learning can help, but whether it will is unpredictable. Riemannian methods reach state-of-the-art performance on several BCI problems, and the authors say they, and tensor methods, deserve more exploration. Shrinkage LDA and random forests work well when training data is scarce. Deep learning had not yet shown a convincing improvement over these methods.

II · The Linear Algebra

MachineryCovariance matrices, two ways

The abstract names its winners but gives no equations. What follows is the standard form of the two methods it singles out, shrinkage LDA and Riemannian classification, because both hinge on the spectrum of one object.

The objects

\(X\in\mathbb R^{C\times T}\)one trial

One trial: \(C\) channels (electrodes) by \(T\) time samples, band-pass filtered to the rhythm of interest so each row has mean roughly 0.

\(\Sigma=\tfrac1T XX^{\top}\)SPD, \(C\times C\)

The trial's spatial covariance. Its diagonal is the power in each channel and its off-diagonal entries are how channels co-vary. It is symmetric positive semidefinite, and positive definite (SPD) once \(T\ge C\) and the rows are independent. Imagined movement shows up as a power change over the motor areas, so it lives in \(\Sigma\).

\(A\in GL(C)\)mixing

Each electrode hears a linear mix of underlying brain sources \(S\) (volume conduction): \(X = AS\), with \(A\) an unknown invertible mixing matrix, different for every head and electrode placement. Covariances then transform by congruence:

$$\Sigma_X=\tfrac1T ASS^{\top}\!A^{\top}=A\,\Sigma_S\,A^{\top}.$$

So the useful information is in \(\Sigma_S\), but we only ever see it through an unknown congruence. Keep that in mind.

Shrinkage LDA: fix the spectrum before you invert it

LDA (linear discriminant analysis) takes a feature vector \(f\in\mathbb R^p\) per trial, for example the log-power of a few filtered channels, and assigns a class by the sign of \(w^{\top}f+b\), where \(\mu_1,\mu_2\) are the two class means, \(S\) is the pooled within-class sample covariance, and

$$w=S^{-1}(\mu_1-\mu_2).$$

With \(n\) training trials and \(p\) not much smaller than \(n\), \(S\)'s eigenvalues are systematically too spread out: the largest are overestimated and the smallest underestimated, possibly to 0. \(S^{-1}\) weights each eigendirection by \(1/\ell_i\), so the noisiest directions get the largest weight. Shrinkage blends \(S\) toward a multiple of the identity, where \(\nu=\operatorname{tr}(S)/p\) is the average eigenvalue and \(\lambda\in[0,1]\):

$$\hat\Sigma=(1-\lambda)S+\lambda\nu I,\qquad S=U\Lambda U^{\top}\ \Rightarrow\ \hat\Sigma=U\big((1-\lambda)\Lambda+\lambda\nu I\big)U^{\top}.$$

The eigenvectors are untouched; each eigenvalue moves linearly toward the mean \(\nu\). That is all shrinkage does, and it bounds the condition number. \(\lambda\) need not be cross-validated: the Ledoit–Wolf formula estimates the error-minimising \(\lambda\) from the training data in closed form.

Tiny example

\(S=\begin{pmatrix}1&0.9\\0.9&1\end{pmatrix}\) has eigenvalues \(1.9\) and \(0.1\) (eigenvectors \((1,\pm1)/\sqrt2\)), so condition number 19. Here \(\nu=1\). With \(\lambda=0.5\), \(\hat\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}1&0.45\\0.45&1\end{pmatrix}\), eigenvalues \(1.45\) and \(0.55\), condition number \(2.6\). The weight on the \((1,-1)\) direction drops from \(1/0.1=10\) to \(1/0.55\approx1.8\).

Riemannian classification: a distance blind to mixing

Riemannian methods skip hand-made features and classify \(\Sigma\) itself. SPD matrices form an open convex cone, not a vector space, and the Frobenius distance \(\|\Sigma_1-\Sigma_2\|_F\) ignores that. The usual choice is the affine-invariant distance:

$$\delta_R(\Sigma_1,\Sigma_2)=\big\|\log\!\big(\Sigma_1^{-1/2}\Sigma_2\Sigma_1^{-1/2}\big)\big\|_F=\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{C}\ln^2\lambda_i\Big)^{1/2},$$

where \(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_C>0\) are the eigenvalues of \(\Sigma_1^{-1}\Sigma_2\), equivalently the generalized eigenvalues of \(\Sigma_2v=\lambda\Sigma_1v\). \(\log\) is the matrix logarithm. It measures how many “factors of \(e\)” the second ellipsoid is stretched relative to the first, direction by direction.

The spectral fact doing the work: invariance under congruence

For any invertible \(A\),

$$(A\Sigma_1A^{\top})^{-1}(A\Sigma_2A^{\top})=A^{-\top}\,\big(\Sigma_1^{-1}\Sigma_2\big)\,A^{\top},$$

which is similar to \(\Sigma_1^{-1}\Sigma_2\), so it has the same eigenvalues and \(\delta_R\) is unchanged. Since the head's mixing acts exactly as a congruence, distances between source covariances equal distances between electrode covariances. The unknown \(A\) drops out. Frobenius distance has no such property.

Tiny example

\(\Sigma_1=I,\ \Sigma_2=\operatorname{diag}(4,1)\). Then \(\|\Sigma_2-\Sigma_1\|_F=3\) and \(\delta_R=\sqrt{\ln^2 4+\ln^2 1}=\ln 4\approx1.386\).

Mix with \(A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\): \(A\Sigma_1A^{\top}=\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\2&2\end{pmatrix}\), \(A\Sigma_2A^{\top}=\begin{pmatrix}16&8\\8&5\end{pmatrix}\). The Frobenius distance is now \(\big\|\begin{pmatrix}12&6\\6&3\end{pmatrix}\big\|_F=15\), five times larger. \(\delta_R\) is still \(\ln4\).

Two classifiers follow. Minimum distance to Riemannian mean (MDM): for each class \(k\), compute the mean \(\bar\Sigma_k\) that minimises the summed squared distance to that class's training covariances (no closed form for more than two matrices; a short fixed-point iteration finds it), then label a new trial by its nearest mean:

$$\bar\Sigma_k=\arg\min_{M\succ0}\sum_{i\in k}\delta_R^2(M,\Sigma_i),\qquad \hat y=\arg\min_k\,\delta_R(\Sigma,\bar\Sigma_k).$$

Tangent space: whiten by the overall mean \(\bar\Sigma\) and take the matrix log, which flattens the cone near \(\bar\Sigma\) into the vector space of symmetric matrices:

$$s=\operatorname{upper}\!\Big(\log\big(\bar\Sigma^{-1/2}\,\Sigma\,\bar\Sigma^{-1/2}\big)\Big)\in\mathbb R^{C(C+1)/2},$$

where “upper” stacks the upper triangle, with off-diagonals scaled by \(\sqrt2\) so that Euclidean norms of \(s\) match \(\delta_R\) to \(\bar\Sigma\). Any linear classifier, such as shrinkage LDA, then runs on \(s\).

IV · Cool Things

ConnectionsWhy the geometry wins

The classic filter uses the same eigenvaluesCSP

The standard pre-2007 pipeline used common spatial patterns (CSP): find channel weightings \(w\) that maximise one class's power relative to the other, i.e. solve \(\Sigma_1w=\mu\,\Sigma_2w\) for the class-mean covariances, and keep the eigenvectors with the most extreme \(\mu\). Those \(\mu\) are exactly the \(\lambda_i\) inside \(\delta_R(\Sigma_2,\Sigma_1)\). CSP keeps a few extreme directions; the Riemannian distance sums \(\ln^2\) over all of them.

Averaging matrices can inflate themswelling

Average \(\operatorname{diag}(4,\tfrac14)\) and \(\operatorname{diag}(\tfrac14,4)\), both of determinant 1. The entrywise mean is \(\operatorname{diag}(2.125,2.125)\), determinant about 4.5: a class mean with far more power than any of its members. The Riemannian mean is \(I\), determinant 1. Here it is just the entrywise geometric mean of the diagonals, and in general the determinant of the Riemannian mean is the geometric mean of the determinants.

Unlabelled adaptation, for freere-centring

The abstract's surprise is that adapting without labels already helps. The invariance shows one simple way it can: take a new session's covariances, compute their mean \(M\) without any labels, and map \(\Sigma\mapsto M^{-1/2}\Sigma M^{-1/2}\). That is a congruence, so it preserves all distances within the session, but it moves the session's centre to \(I\). Sessions and users drifting apart then line up on a common origin.

Why deep nets hadn't wontens of trials

A calibration session yields a few dozen trials per class. MDM has one SPD mean per class to estimate and builds in the invariance; a network must learn its own invariances from the same handful of examples. That gap, more than any flaw in deep learning, is the likely reason for the abstract's verdict.

V · If You Remember One Thing

The one thing to remember

The head mixes brain sources by an unknown invertible matrix, which acts on covariance matrices as a congruence; the eigenvalues of \(\Sigma_1^{-1}\Sigma_2\) survive every congruence, so the Riemannian classifiers, which measure distance with those eigenvalues alone, never have to learn the mixing, and that is a big part of why they reached the state of the art.
