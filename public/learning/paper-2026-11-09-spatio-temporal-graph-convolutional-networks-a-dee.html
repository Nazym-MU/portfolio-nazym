Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Networks: A Deep Learning Framework for Traffic Forecasting

Bing Yu, Haoteng Yin, Zhanxing Zhu · 2017 · arXiv:1709.04875

The paper forecasts traffic speed at hundreds of road sensors by treating each moment's readings as a signal on the road network, filtering it with polynomials of the network's Laplacian matrix in space and with plain convolutions in time, so the whole model trains in parallel instead of step by step.

I · In 60 Seconds

Problem1 hour in, 45 min out

Sensors along highways report the average speed of passing cars every 5 minutes. Given the last hour of readings (12 per sensor) at every sensor, predict the speeds 15, 30 and 45 minutes ahead. Nearby sensors are coupled through the roads: a jam spreads back along the road that feeds it, not across empty space.

Image-style convolutions need the data on a regular grid, and sensors are not on one. The usual alternative for time series, recurrent networks, read the sequence one step at a time: slow to train, because the steps can't be computed in parallel.

Ideaall convolution

Make the sensors the nodes of a weighted graph, so one 5-minute snapshot is a vector with one entry per sensor. For space, filter that vector with a polynomial in the graph Laplacian, a matrix built from the road graph; such a filter mixes each sensor only with sensors a few road hops away. For time, slide an ordinary short convolution along each sensor's history.

Stack these as time → graph → time “sandwich” blocks. Nothing is recurrent, so every time step is processed at once.

Result14× faster training

On 228 Los Angeles-area highway sensors, the average absolute error of the 45-minute forecast is 3.57 (speeds in the data run from about 20 to 70), against 4.01 for the best earlier graph model, which pairs graph filters with a recurrent network, and 4.16 for a standard recurrent network that ignores the graph.

It trains in 272 seconds instead of 3,824, with about two-thirds as many learned numbers (454,000).

II · The Linear Algebra

MachineryPolynomials of the Laplacian

The objects

\(v_t\in\mathbb R^{n}\)a graph signal

The speeds at all \(n\) sensors at time step \(t\): one number per node of the graph. The model sees \(M=12\) consecutive snapshots and predicts \(H = 3, 6\) or \(9\) steps ahead.

\(W\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}\)symmetric, sparse, ≥ 0

The weighted adjacency matrix: \(w_{ij}\) is how strongly sensors \(i\) and \(j\) are linked. For the California data it comes from road distance \(d_{ij}\) through a Gaussian, cut off below a threshold \(\epsilon\):

$$w_{ij}=\begin{cases}e^{-d_{ij}^2/\sigma^2} & i\ne j,\ e^{-d_{ij}^2/\sigma^2}\ge\epsilon\\[2pt] 0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\qquad \sigma^2=10,\ \epsilon=0.5 .$$

So \(W\) is symmetric, nonnegative, zero on the diagonal, and sparse: distant pairs are exactly 0. (The Beijing dataset uses a directed graph, where \(W\) isn't symmetric and the spectral story below doesn't strictly apply; the paper doesn't address this.)

\(D=\operatorname{diag}(d_i),\ d_i=\sum_j w_{ij}\)degrees

Each sensor's total link weight, on a diagonal.

\(L=I_n-D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}\)symmetric PSD, spectrum ⊂ [0,2]

The normalized graph Laplacian (\(I_n\) is the identity). It has the same sparsity pattern as \(W\) plus the diagonal. Its quadratic form measures how much a signal changes across edges:

$$x^{\top}Lx=\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}w_{ij}\Big(\tfrac{x_i}{\sqrt{d_i}}-\tfrac{x_j}{\sqrt{d_j}}\Big)^2\ \ge 0 .$$

So \(L\) is positive semidefinite. Applying \((a-b)^2\le 2a^2+2b^2\) to each term gives \(x^\top Lx\le 2\|x\|^2\), so every eigenvalue lies in \([0,2]\). The eigenvalue 0 has eigenvector \(D^{1/2}\mathbf 1\) (once per connected piece of the graph).

The spectral definition of a graph convolution

By the spectral theorem, \(L=U\Lambda U^{\top}\) with \(U\) orthogonal (columns are orthonormal eigenvectors) and \(\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1\le\dots\le\lambda_n)\). By the quadratic form above, eigenvectors with small \(\lambda\) vary little across edges and those near 2 flip sign across them, so the \(\lambda_k\) play the role of frequencies. \(U^{\top}x\) is the graph Fourier transform of \(x\). A convolution is then defined as multiplying each frequency by its own gain, a diagonal filter \(\Theta(\Lambda)\):

$$\Theta *_{\mathcal G} x \;=\; U\,\Theta(\Lambda)\,U^{\top}x .$$

Taken literally, this is unusable at scale. Computing \(U\) costs \(O(n^3)\); \(U\) is dense, so each filter application costs \(O(n^2)\); the filter has \(n\) free gains; and a generic choice of gains mixes every sensor with every other one.

The fact that does the work: restrict \(\Theta\) to polynomials

If the gain is a polynomial in the eigenvalue, \(\Theta(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\theta_k\lambda^k\), then because \(U^{\top}U=I\),

$$U\,\Theta(\Lambda)\,U^{\top}=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\theta_k\,U\Lambda^kU^{\top}=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\theta_k\,L^k .$$

The eigenvectors drop out. The spectral filter is a polynomial in the sparse matrix \(L\), applied by repeated sparse matrix–vector products and never by diagonalising. Three consequences:

Localised. \((L^k)_{ij}=0\) whenever \(i\) and \(j\) are more than \(k\) edges apart, since every term of the product is a walk of length \(k\). A degree-\((K-1)\) filter mixes each sensor only with its \((K-1)\)-hop neighbourhood. \(K\) is the filter's “kernel size”.

Cheap. \(K\) learned numbers per filter, and \(O(K|\mathcal E|)\) work, where \(|\mathcal E|\) is the number of edges.

Still spectral. It is exactly the filter with gain \(\Theta(\lambda)\) at frequency \(\lambda\), so the Fourier picture still describes what it does.

Tiny example: a 3-sensor road

Path graph 1–2–3 with unit weights: \(D=\operatorname{diag}(1,2,1)\) and

$$L=\begin{pmatrix}1&-\tfrac1{\sqrt2}&0\\-\tfrac1{\sqrt2}&1&-\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\\0&-\tfrac1{\sqrt2}&1\end{pmatrix},\quad \lambda=0,\,1,\,2,\quad u=\tfrac12\!\begin{pmatrix}1\\\sqrt2\\1\end{pmatrix}\!,\ \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\!\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix}\!,\ \tfrac12\!\begin{pmatrix}1\\-\sqrt2\\1\end{pmatrix}\!.$$

The smoothest mode has one sign everywhere, the middle one changes sign once, and the top one alternates. \(\lambda_{\max}=2\) exactly because a path is bipartite.

Put a spike at sensor 1, \(e_1=(1,0,0)^\top\). Then \(Le_1=(1,-\tfrac1{\sqrt2},0)^\top\) reaches only its neighbour, and \(L^2e_1=(\tfrac32,-\sqrt2,\tfrac12)^\top\) first reaches sensor 3, two hops away.

Chebyshev basis, then first order

Monomials \(L^k\) are a badly conditioned basis. The paper uses Chebyshev polynomials \(T_k\), defined by \(T_0=1,\ T_1(x)=x,\ T_k=2xT_{k-1}-T_{k-2}\), with \(|T_k|\le1\) on \([-1,1]\). To use them, it first shifts the spectrum into \([-1,1]\) with \(\tilde L=2L/\lambda_{\max}-I_n\), where \(\lambda_{\max}\) is \(L\)'s largest eigenvalue:

$$\Theta *_{\mathcal G} x\approx\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\theta_k\,T_k(\tilde L)\,x,\qquad \bar x_k=2\tilde L\,\bar x_{k-1}-\bar x_{k-2}.$$

Here \(\bar x_k=T_k(\tilde L)x\), so each new term costs one sparse product. The paper's main model, STGCN(Cheb), uses \(K=3\): two hops.

The cheaper variant, STGCN(1st), keeps \(K=2\) and assumes \(\lambda_{\max}\approx2\), so \(\tilde L\approx L-I=-D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}\). Tying the two coefficients as \(\theta=\theta_0=-\theta_1\) leaves one number per filter:

$$\Theta *_{\mathcal G} x\approx\theta\,\big(I_n+D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}\big)x\ \ \leadsto\ \ \theta\,\tilde D^{-1/2}\tilde W\tilde D^{-1/2}x,\qquad \tilde W=W+I_n,\ \tilde D_{ii}=\textstyle\sum_j\tilde W_{ij}.$$

The paper writes the last step as “=”, but it is a replacement, the “renormalisation trick” of Kipf and Welling: add a self-loop to every node, then normalise. The reason is spectral. \(I+D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}=2I-L\) has eigenvalues \(2-\lambda\in[0,2]\). Stacking these layers multiplies such matrices, so components with eigenvalue near 2 grow like \(2^{\text{layers}}\). The renormalised matrix has spectrum in \((-1,1]\), so its powers stay bounded. Each layer reaches one hop, so stacking layers buys the reach.

Tiny example: the same path, before and after

\(I+D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}\) has eigenvalues \(2,1,0\). With self-loops, \(\tilde D=\operatorname{diag}(2,3,2)\) and

$$\tilde D^{-1/2}\tilde W\tilde D^{-1/2}=\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac12&\tfrac1{\sqrt6}&0\\\tfrac1{\sqrt6}&\tfrac13&\tfrac1{\sqrt6}\\0&\tfrac1{\sqrt6}&\tfrac12\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{eigenvalues } 1,\ \tfrac12,\ -\tfrac16 .$$

Eigenvectors: \((\sqrt2,\sqrt3,\sqrt2)\), \((1,0,-1)\) and \((\sqrt3,-2\sqrt2,\sqrt3)\). Check the last: the trace is \(\tfrac43=1+\tfrac12-\tfrac16\). Ten stacked layers scale the top component by \(2^{10}=1024\) before the trick and by \(1\) after.

Many channels, many time steps: a Kronecker structure

Inside the network each sensor carries \(C_i\) features (“channels”), so a snapshot is \(X\in\mathbb R^{n\times C_i}\), and a layer outputs \(C_o\) channels. The paper's channel-wise formula \(y_j=\sum_i\Theta_{i,j}(L)x_i\) is, in matrix form,

$$Y=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}T_k(\tilde L)\,X\,\Theta_k,\qquad \operatorname{vec}(Y)=\sum_{k}\big(\Theta_k^{\top}\otimes T_k(\tilde L)\big)\operatorname{vec}(X),$$

with learned \(\Theta_k\in\mathbb R^{C_i\times C_o}\). The graph polynomial multiplies on the left, across sensors; the channel mixing multiplies on the right. The same \(\Theta_k\) is applied to every one of the 12 time steps.

Time is handled symmetrically. Along each sensor's history the model applies a 1-D convolution of width \(K_t=3\) without padding, which is a banded Toeplitz matrix acting on the time axis, shared across all sensors. It produces \(2C_o\) channels, split into halves \(P\) and \(Q\), and outputs \(P\odot\sigma(Q)\), where \(\sigma\) is the logistic sigmoid and \(\odot\) is entrywise product. This is a gated linear unit: \(\sigma(Q)\in(0,1)\) acts as a learned, data-dependent mask on \(P\). Each such layer shortens the sequence by \(K_t-1=2\).

One block is time, then graph, then time, with \(\mathrm{ReLU}(z)=\max(z,0)\) after the graph step:

$$v^{l+1}=\Gamma_1^{l} *_{\mathcal T}\mathrm{ReLU}\big(\Theta^{l} *_{\mathcal G}(\Gamma_0^{l} *_{\mathcal T} v^{l})\big),\qquad v^{l}\in\mathbb R^{M\times n\times C}.$$

Here \(\Gamma_0^l,\Gamma_1^l\) are the two time kernels of block \(l\) and \(\Theta^l\) its graph kernel. The linear parts act on different axes of the tensor (time, sensors, channels), so they commute; only the gates and ReLU couple them. A general linear map on one 12-step, 228-sensor input would have \((12\cdot228)^2\approx7.5\) million entries per channel pair. The factored version needs \(K\) graph coefficients and \(K_t\) time coefficients.

IV · Cool Things

FindingsAnd connections

On a ring road, graph Fourier is Fouriercirculant

For a cycle of \(n\) sensors, \(L=I-\tfrac12W\) is circulant, so the discrete Fourier basis diagonalises it, with \(\lambda_k=1-\cos(2\pi k/n)\). A degree-\((K-1)\) polynomial in \(L\) is then a symmetric 1-D convolution kernel of width \(2K-1\). The graph construction contains ordinary convolution as a special case.

The filter lives in a Krylov subspace\(\mathcal K_K(\tilde L,x)\)

\(\sum_k\theta_kT_k(\tilde L)x\in\operatorname{span}\{x,\tilde Lx,\dots,\tilde L^{K-1}x\}\), the same space conjugate gradient and Lanczos search. Those methods choose the combination to solve a system or find eigenvalues; here the coefficients are learned by gradient descent to predict traffic.

No recurrence, ten times less training time272 s vs 3,824 s

On the 1,026-sensor graph, training took 1,927 s for STGCN against 19,512 s for the graph-plus-recurrent baseline (GCGRU). The baseline also had to halve its batch size to fit on one GPU. STGCN uses about 95% fewer learned numbers than a plain recurrent network (FC-LSTM).

One coefficient per filter barely hurts3.57 vs 3.79

The first-order model (one \(\theta\) per channel pair, one hop per layer) has a 45-minute error of 3.79 against 3.57 for the two-hop Chebyshev model, and trains about 20% faster on the large graph (1,554 s vs 1,927 s).

V · If You Remember One Thing

The one thing to remember

A spectral filter \(U\Theta(\Lambda)U^\top\) whose gain is a degree-\((K-1)\) polynomial equals that polynomial in the sparse Laplacian \(L\), so it needs no eigenvectors, has \(K\) parameters, and mixes each sensor only with sensors within \(K-1\) road hops. That identity is what makes convolution on a road network practical.
