FoldingNet

Point Cloud Auto-Encoder via Deep Grid Deformation

Yaoqing Yang, Chen Feng, Yiru Shen et al. · 2017 · arXiv:1712.07262

The paper teaches a network to squeeze a 3D shape, given only as an unordered cloud of points, into 512 numbers, and to rebuild the shape from those numbers by folding a flat square grid of points into the object's surface.

I · In 60 Seconds

Problemno grid, no order

A point cloud is a 3D shape stored as a list of surface points, each an \((x,y,z)\) position, as produced by laser scanners or by sampling a CAD model. Unlike an image, it has no pixel grid and no natural order: shuffle the list and it is the same shape. Image networks rely on that grid, so they don't apply directly.

The goal here is unsupervised: learn, without any labels, a short vector (the codeword) that summarises each shape. The tool is an auto-encoder: an encoder compresses the shape to the codeword, a decoder rebuilds the shape from it, and both are trained to make the rebuild match the input. The earlier point-cloud decoder simply output all 2048 × 3 coordinates from a big fully connected network.

Ideafold a 2D grid

Object surfaces are two-dimensional, so build them from something two-dimensional. The decoder starts from a fixed 45 × 45 grid of points in a flat square. It attaches the same codeword to every grid point and passes each (grid point, codeword) pair through one small shared network that outputs a 3D position. It does this twice (“two folds”). The codeword decides how the sheet is bent, stretched and creased into a chair or an aeroplane.

The encoder respects the no-order rule: it works point by point, pools over each point's 16 nearest neighbours, then takes a max over all points, so shuffling the input cannot change the codeword.

Result88.4% · 7% of params

The auto-encoder is trained on ShapeNet (57,447 unlabelled 3D models). Its codewords are then computed for ModelNet40, a separate collection of 40 object categories, and a linear classifier (a linear support vector machine, one hyperplane per class) is trained on them. It reaches 88.4% accuracy, beating every earlier unsupervised method (best previous: 85.7%).

The folding decoder has 1.05 million parameters, about 7% of the 15.2 million in the fully connected decoder it replaces, and gives both lower reconstruction error and higher accuracy. With only 1% of the labels (98 shapes) the linear classifier still gets over 55%.

II · The Linear Algebra

MachineryRows, permutations, a rank-one bias

Everything in FoldingNet is a matrix whose rows are points, and every layer either acts on each row separately with shared weights, or pools over a set of rows. That single design rule is what makes the order of points irrelevant on the way in and lets a grid supply an order on the way out.

The objects

\(X\in\mathbb R^{n\times 3}\)input, n = 2048

One row per input point. The rows form a set: \(X\) and \(PX\) describe the same shape for any \(n\times n\) permutation matrix \(P\) (one 1 in each row and column, so orthogonal).

\(C_v\in\mathbb R^{3\times 3}\)symmetric PSD

For each point \(v\), the covariance of its neighbourhood: \(v\) and its 16 nearest neighbours, centred at their mean \(\bar p\), \(C_v \propto \sum (p-\bar p)(p-\bar p)^{\top}\). Its 9 entries (only 6 distinct, by symmetry) are appended to the point's position, giving an \(n\times 12\) matrix.

\(A\)sparse, 16 per row

Adjacency matrix of the 16-nearest-neighbour graph on the input points, rebuilt from the positions for each shape. Each row has 16 ones out of 2048. It need not be symmetric, since “\(j\) is among \(i\)'s 16 nearest” does not imply the reverse. \(\mathcal N(i)\) denotes row \(i\)'s neighbours.

\(\theta\in\mathbb R^{512}\)codeword

The encoder's output, the whole shape in 512 numbers.

\(U\in\mathbb R^{m\times 2}\)fixed grid, m = 2025

A 45 × 45 grid of points in a square centred at the origin (2025 is the square nearest to 2048). Never trained. The output \(\hat S\in\mathbb R^{m\times 3}\) has one row per grid point.

Encoder: equivariant layers, then one invariant pool

The encoder runs: a 3-layer network applied to each row (\(n\times12\to n\times64\)), two graph layers (\(\to n\times1024\)), a max over all rows (\(\to 1024\)), and a 2-layer network (\(\to\theta\)). A graph layer replaces each feature by its maximum over the point's neighbours, then mixes features with a learned matrix \(W_g\) (the paper calls it \(K\)):

$$\big(A_{\max}(X)\big)_{ij}=\mathrm{ReLU}\Big(\max_{k\in\mathcal N(i)} x_{kj}\Big),\qquad Y=A_{\max}(X)\,W_g .$$

Compare a standard graph convolution \(Y=AXW_g\): the sum over neighbours \(\sum_k A_{ik}x_{kj}\) becomes a max over neighbours. Both are equivariant: permuting the points permutes the rows of the output the same way. For the per-row network \(f_1\), \(f_1(PL)=Pf_1(L)\) because it never looks across rows. For the graph layer, permuting the points relabels the graph (\(A\mapsto PAP^{\top}\)), so \(A_{\max}(PX)=P\,A_{\max}(X)\). The final max over rows is invariant: \(\max_i (PY)_{ij}=\max_i Y_{ij}\). Composing, \(\theta(PX)=\theta(X)\), the paper's Theorem 3.1.

Why the covariance is worth 9 extra inputs: its spectrum is the local dimension

\(C_v\) is symmetric positive semidefinite, so \(C_v=Q\Lambda Q^{\top}\) with orthonormal eigenvectors \(q_1,q_2,q_3\) and \(\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\lambda_3\ge0\). Its rank is the dimension of the affine span of the neighbourhood. On a smooth surface, \(\lambda_3\approx0\) and \(q_3\) is the surface normal. Along a thin edge or a chair leg, \(\lambda_2\approx\lambda_3\approx0\). (The paper just feeds the 9 raw entries and lets the network use them; this is why they are informative.)

Line: neighbours \((0,0,0),(1,1,0),(2,2,0)\), mean \((1,1,0)\). Deviations \(\pm(1,1,0)\) and \(0\), so \(C=\tfrac13\begin{pmatrix}2&2&0\\2&2&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\), eigenvalues \(\tfrac43,0,0\), \(q_1=(1,1,0)/\sqrt2\). Rank 1.

Sheet: neighbours \((0,0,0),(1,0,0),(0,1,0),(1,1,0)\), mean \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0)\). Deviations \((\pm\tfrac12,\pm\tfrac12,0)\), whose \(xy\) products cancel, so \(C=\mathrm{diag}(\tfrac14,\tfrac14,0)\). Rank 2, normal \(q_3=e_3\).

Decoder: the codeword is a rank-one bias

Write \(\mathbf 1_m\) for the all-ones column. “Replicate the codeword \(m\) times and concatenate” is the matrix \([\,U,\ \mathbf 1_m\theta^{\top}]\in\mathbb R^{m\times514}\), and \(\mathbf 1_m\theta^{\top}\) has rank one. A fold applies one 3-layer network \(f\) to every row. Its first layer, with \(d_1\) hidden units, weights split as \(W_U\in\mathbb R^{2\times d_1}\) and \(W_\theta\in\mathbb R^{512\times d_1}\), and bias \(b\in\mathbb R^{d_1}\), computes

$$[\,U,\ \mathbf 1_m\theta^{\top}]\begin{pmatrix}W_U\\ W_\theta\end{pmatrix}+\mathbf 1_m b^{\top} \;=\; U W_U \;+\; \mathbf 1_m\big(b+W_\theta^{\top}\theta\big)^{\top}.$$

So the codeword never multiplies the grid. It only shifts the first layer's bias, identically for every grid point. The decoder is one fixed family of maps \(u\mapsto f(u;\theta)\) from the plane to \(\mathbb R^3\), and \(\theta\) picks a member. Two folds:

$$Z=f_1\big([\,U,\ \mathbf 1_m\theta^{\top}]\big)\in\mathbb R^{m\times3},\qquad \hat S=f_2\big([\,Z,\ \mathbf 1_m\theta^{\top}]\big)\in\mathbb R^{m\times3}.$$

Because weights are shared across rows, the parameter count does not depend on \(m\). The fully connected decoder it replaces has layers \(512\to1024\to2048\to2048\cdot3\): \(512\cdot1024+1024\cdot2048+2048\cdot6144\approx15.2\)M weights, most in the last matrix, one column per output coordinate. The folding decoder has 1.05M.

Training minimises the (extended) Chamfer distance between the input set \(S\) and the output set \(\hat S\): the larger of the two average nearest-neighbour distances,

$$d_{CH}(S,\hat S)=\max\Big\{\frac1{|S|}\sum_{x\in S}\min_{\hat x\in\hat S}\|x-\hat x\|_2,\ \ \frac1{|\hat S|}\sum_{\hat x\in\hat S}\min_{x\in S}\|\hat x-x\|_2\Big\}.$$

The first term says every input point is near some output point (nothing missed), the second that every output point is near some input point (nothing invented). It compares sets, so it ignores order and allows \(m=2025\ne n=2048\).

Why one fold can make anything: a selector built from a Kronecker product

Theorem 3.2 says a single fold with a 2-layer network (one hidden layer) can output any \(m\)-point cloud \(S\in\mathbb R^{m\times3}\), entries in \([-1,1]\), by choosing \(\theta\). The construction is a lookup table. Take \(\theta=\mathrm{vec}(S^{\top})=(s_{11},s_{12},s_{13},s_{21},\dots,s_{m3})\in\mathbb R^{3m}\), and let grid point \(i\) be \(p_i=\delta\,(2\beta_i+1,\ 2\gamma_i+1)\) with integers \(|\beta_i|,|\gamma_i|<M\). The odd multiples of \(\delta\) guarantee no coordinate is 0. The hidden layer has \(3m\) units, one per entry of \(S\). With \(D=\mathrm{diag}(u^2,u)\), unit \((j,k)\) computes

$$h_{jk}=p_j^{\top}D\,(p_i-p_j)+s_{jk},\qquad z_{jk}=\begin{cases}h_{jk}&|h_{jk}|<c\\0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\qquad w=\big(\mathbf 1_m^{\top}\otimes I_3\big)\,z .$$

This is affine in the input \((p_i,\theta)\): the weights on \(p_i\) are \(p_j^{\top}D\), the bias is \(-p_j^{\top}Dp_j\), and the weight block on \(\theta\) is \(I_{3m}\). The output matrix \(\mathbf 1_m^{\top}\otimes I_3\) (\(3\times3m\)) sums the \(m\) groups. The window activation \(z\) can be assembled from ReLUs.

The key fact: \(p_j^{\top}D(p_i-p_j)=u^2\delta^2 m_1+u\delta^2 m_2\) with integers \(m_1=2(2\beta_j+1)(\beta_i-\beta_j)\) and \(m_2=2(2\gamma_j+1)(\gamma_i-\gamma_j)\). Since \(2\beta_j+1\) is odd, hence nonzero, \(m_1=0\) iff the \(x\)-coordinates agree. Choose \(u\delta^2>c+1\), \(u>8M^2+4M+1\) and \(c>1\). Then:

  • \(i=j\): \(h=s_{jk}\), and \(|s_{jk}|\le1<c\), so it passes.
  • \(i\ne j\): \(|h|>c\), so it is zeroed. If the \(x\)'s differ, the \(u^2\) term beats the largest possible \(u\) term, \(|m_2|<8M^2+4M\); this is why \(D\) weights \(x\) by \(u^2\) but \(y\) only by \(u\). If only the \(y\)'s differ, \(u\delta^2|m_2|\) alone exceeds \(c+1\).

So \(z=(e_i\otimes\mathbf 1_3)\odot\theta\), with \(e_i\) the \(i\)-th standard basis vector and \(\odot\) entrywise product, and \(w=(\mathbf 1_m^{\top}\otimes I_3)z=S^{\top}e_i\), row \(i\) of \(S\). The grid coordinate is decoded into a one-hot index, which reads one row out of \(\theta\).

Tiny example

\(\delta=1\), two grid points \(p_1=(-1,1)\), \(p_2=(1,1)\) (\(\beta=-1,0\); \(\gamma=0\); \(M=2\)). Then \(u>8\cdot4+8+1=41\), so take \(u=42\), \(c=2\). Target \(s_1=(0.5,-0.2,0.9)\), \(s_2=(-0.3,0.7,0.1)\).

Feed grid point 1. Group \(j=1\): \(p_1^{\top}D(p_1-p_1)=0\), so \(h=s_1=(0.5,-0.2,0.9)\), all inside \((-2,2)\), and all pass. Group \(j=2\): \(p_2^{\top}D(p_1-p_2)=(1,1)\,\mathrm{diag}(1764,42)\,(-2,0)^{\top}=-3528\), so \(h\approx-3528+s_2\), and all are zeroed. Output: \(w=s_1+0=(0.5,-0.2,0.9)\). ✓

The paper is candid that this is existence only: it needs \(3m\) hidden units and a codeword of length \(3m=6075\) that is literally the data, whereas the trained network uses 512 numbers and reconstructs approximately.

Two hidden units just carry \(u\) through; the other four each switch on across a line \(n_r\cdot u=t_r\) in the grid and push the sheet up or down beyond it, which makes a crease. A 3-number codeword \(\theta\) enters exactly as in the paper, through the bias: \(t=t_0+W_\theta^{\top}\theta\).

Try: move one slider at a time, and drag the right panel sideways to turn the sheet. Notice: on the left, the crease lines only slide parallel to themselves; they never rotate. With one hidden layer, a bias shift can move a hyperplane but not turn it. The sheet is always piecewise flat, and neighbouring grid points stay neighbours: the filled dot and the open circle mark the same two corners in both panels.

IV · Cool Things

FindingsFrom the paper and its appendix

The grid's dimension should match the data's1D < 2D ≈ 3D

Same network, different starting grids (accuracy on ModelNet40, then Chamfer loss): a 1D line of points gives 86.71% and 0.0355, a 2D square 88.25% and 0.0296, a 3D cube 88.41% and 0.0284. Going from 1D to 2D helps a lot; 2D to 3D barely, because the shapes are surfaces. A 2D square of random points does worse (87.12%, 0.0321) than the regular grid; the authors blame uneven local density.

“Cuts” are really stretchescontinuity

A fold is a composition of affine maps and ReLUs, so it is continuous, and the image of the square is connected. The decoder can still make a table with four separate legs. It stretches a thin strip of the grid across the gap so far that no grid point lands there, and the Chamfer distance only sees the sampled points. The paper's failures fit this picture: holes in chair backs and aeroplane winglets go missing.

The graph layer is a tropical matrix product(max, +)

In max-plus algebra, “addition” is \(\max\) and “multiplication” is \(+\). Set \(\tilde A_{ik}=0\) for neighbours and \(-\infty\) otherwise. Then \(\max_k(\tilde A_{ik}+x_{kj})\) is exactly the neighbourhood max, so \(A_{\max}(X)=\mathrm{ReLU}(\tilde A\otimes X)\). It is the ordinary graph convolution \(AX\) with the semiring swapped. A max ignores every neighbour except the largest, so a stray point changes nothing unless it wins, whereas it always shifts a sum. With 5% of points moved at random, removing the graph layers costs about 2 points of accuracy, while the full encoder barely changes.

Order for freea mesh, almost

The input has no order, but every output point is labelled by the grid point it came from, so neighbours on the grid are (usually) neighbours on the surface. The reconstruction comes with a parametrisation of the surface by the square. The paper's colour-coded figures use this: one colour gradient across the grid stays smooth on the folded chair. A fully connected decoder's 2048 outputs have no such structure.

V · If You Remember One Thing

The one thing to remember

Treat a shape as a 2D sheet: one small network, shared by every point of a flat grid and steered by a codeword that enters only as a rank-one bias \(\mathbf 1_m\theta^{\top}\), folds the sheet into the surface with 7% of the parameters of a decoder that outputs coordinates directly, and learns better shape features doing it.
