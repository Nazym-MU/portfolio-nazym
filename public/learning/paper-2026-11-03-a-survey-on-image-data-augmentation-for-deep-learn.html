Data Augmentation

A Survey on Image Data Augmentation for Deep Learning

Connor Shorten & Taghi M. Khoshgoftaar · 2019 · J. Big Data 6:60 · ~12,700 citations · doi:10.1186/s40537-019-0197-0

Image classifiers overfit when labelled images are scarce. This survey catalogues the ways to make more training images out of the ones you have, from flips and crops to images faked by a second network, and collects the reported gains.

I · In 60 Seconds

Problem182 scans

A convolutional neural network (CNN), the standard image classifier, has millions of weights. Given too few labelled images it overfits: it memorises the training set and does worse on new images. Medical imaging is the extreme case. One liver-lesion study the survey covers had 182 CT scans in total.

Ideawarp or synthesise

Data augmentation means applying changes that shouldn't alter the label (a mirrored cat is still a cat) and training on the results too. The survey splits the methods into two groups. Data warping transforms an existing image and keeps its label: flips, crops, rotations, colour shifts, blurs, blanking out a random patch, adversarial noise and style transfer. Oversampling creates new examples: blending two images, interpolating between examples inside a network's internal representation, and samples from a GAN (generative adversarial network: one network makes fake images while a second learns to tell them from real ones).

Resultcrops win

AlexNet, the 2012 network that started the deep-learning boom in vision, took random 224×224 crops of 256×256 photos and their mirror images. That is \(32^2\) crop positions × 2 = 2048 times more data, and it cut error by over a point. On Caltech101 (8,421 photos in 101 object categories), cropping alone raised top-1 accuracy from 48.1% to 62.0%. Flips, rotations and colour changes each added only 1–3 points.

Blanking a random rectangle cut error on CIFAR-10 (tiny 32×32 photos in 10 classes) from 5.17% to 4.31%. Averaging two images' pixels and keeping the first one's label cut error on a 1,000-image subset of CIFAR-10 from 43.1% to 31.0%. In the liver study, GAN-made lesions raised sensitivity from 78.6% to 85.7% and specificity from 88.4% to 92.4%. The survey's caveat: no augmentation creates what the data lacks. Photos of bulldogs alone will never yield a golden retriever.

II · The Linear Algebra

MachineryStructured maps on pixel space

The survey has no equations of its own, but almost every “basic image manipulation” it lists is an affine map with a very particular matrix. Write an image of height \(H\), width \(W\) and 3 colour channels (red, green, blue) as one long vector \(x\in\mathbb R^n\), \(n=3HW\). Stack it channel by channel, each channel row by row. For CIFAR-10, \(n=3072\). An augmentation is then

$$\tilde x \;=\; Mx + b,$$

where \(M\) is an \(n\times n\) matrix (or \(m\times n\) for a crop) and \(b\) a fixed offset. Training on augmented data means minimising the average loss over random draws of \((M,b)\) rather than the loss on \(x\) alone.

The catalogue, by matrix structure

Flip, 180° turn, PatchShufflepermutation

Each output pixel is some input pixel, so \(M=P\) is a permutation matrix: orthogonal, \(P^\top P=I\), with eigenvalues on the unit circle. A horizontal flip is \(I_3\otimes I_H\otimes J_W\), where \(J_W\) is the \(W\times W\) matrix with ones on the anti-diagonal and \(\otimes\) is the Kronecker product. It is also symmetric with \(P^2=I\), so its eigenvalues are \(\pm1\). PatchShuffle randomly permutes the pixels inside small windows; with 2×2 windows, each shuffled with probability 0.05, it cut CIFAR-10 error from 6.33% to 5.66%.

Crop, translate with fillselection

A crop keeps some coordinates, so it is \(S\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}\) made of rows of the identity. Then \(SS^\top=I_m\), and \(S^\top S\) is the diagonal projection onto the kept pixels. A shift that pads the vacated border with a constant is a permutation with some rows zeroed, plus \(b\) for the fill. Both have rank \(\lt n\): they throw information away.

Random erasing / cutoutdiagonal projection

Blank out a random patch and fill it: \(\tilde x = Dx+(I-D)r\). Here \(D\) is diagonal with entries 0 on the patch and 1 elsewhere (an orthogonal projection), and \(r\) is the fill. The survey reports random values as the best fill.

Colour transforms\(C\otimes I_{HW}\)

A colour change that treats each pixel the same applies one \(3\times3\) matrix \(C\) to every pixel's RGB vector, so \(M=C\otimes I_{HW}\). Brightness is \(b=\beta\mathbf 1\). Isolating a channel uses \(C=e_1e_1^\top\), a rank-1 projection. Converting to greyscale is \(w^\top\otimes I_{HW}\), with \(w\) the luminance weights. The survey quotes a ~3% accuracy drop for greyscale versus colour on ImageNet.

Kernel filters (blur, sharpen)block circulant

Sliding a small kernel \(k\) over the image is convolution. With wrap-around boundaries, each channel's matrix \(K\) is block circulant with circulant blocks. Every such matrix is diagonalised by the 2-D discrete Fourier transform \(F\): \(K=F^{*}\operatorname{diag}(\hat k)F\), where \(\hat k\) is the kernel's Fourier transform. A symmetric blur kernel gives real eigenvalues that shrink toward 0 at high frequency (a low-pass filter). Sharpening pushes high-frequency eigenvalues above 1.

Mixing (SamplePairing), noise\(\tfrac12 I\), \(I\)

SamplePairing averages \(x\) with another random training image \(x'\) and keeps \(x\)'s label: \(\tilde x=\tfrac12x+\tfrac12x'\), so the linear part is \(\tfrac12 I\) and \(x'\) acts as the offset. With \(N\) images, that gives \(N^2+N\) possible training inputs. Noise injection is \(M=I\) with a random Gaussian \(b\).

Tiny example: why a blur is a low-pass filter

Take three pixels on a loop and the kernel \((\tfrac14,\tfrac12,\tfrac14)\). The matrix is circulant and symmetric:

$$K=\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac12&\tfrac14&\tfrac14\\[2pt]\tfrac14&\tfrac12&\tfrac14\\[2pt]\tfrac14&\tfrac14&\tfrac12\end{pmatrix},\qquad K\mathbf 1=\mathbf 1,\qquad K\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\0\end{pmatrix}=\tfrac14\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\0\end{pmatrix}.$$

The flat image (eigenvalue 1) passes untouched, and every oscillating pattern (eigenvalue \(\tfrac14\)) is damped fourfold. These are exactly the Fourier eigenvalues \(\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cos(2\pi j/3)\) for \(j=0,1,2\).

The one spectral step that does real work: “Fancy PCA” colour jitter

The survey credits AlexNet's colour augmentation, “PCA color augmentation” (“Fancy PCA” in its Table 1), but doesn't give the recipe. From the AlexNet paper: collect the RGB vector \(c_p\in\mathbb R^3\) of every pixel \(p\) in the training set, with mean \(\bar c\). Form their \(3\times3\) covariance, which is symmetric positive semidefinite, and diagonalise it:

$$\Sigma=\frac1N\sum_{p}(c_p-\bar c)(c_p-\bar c)^{\top}=P\Lambda P^{\top},\qquad P=[\,p_1\ p_2\ p_3\,],\ \ \lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\lambda_3\ge0 .$$

Then, each time an image is used, draw three scalars \(\alpha_i\sim\mathcal N(0,0.1^2)\) and add the same vector to every pixel of that image:

$$c_p\;\longmapsto\;c_p+P\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_1\lambda_1\\ \alpha_2\lambda_2\\ \alpha_3\lambda_3\end{pmatrix},\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\text{shift})=P\,(0.1^2\Lambda^2)\,P^{\top}=0.01\,\Sigma^2 .$$

Why the eigendecomposition matters: the jitter follows the directions along which colour actually varies across natural photos, instead of adding isotropic RGB noise that produces tints no camera would record. The subtlety is in the last equation. Scaling by \(\lambda_i\) rather than \(\sqrt{\lambda_i}\) gives the shifts covariance \(\Sigma^2\), not \(\Sigma\), so the dominant direction is exaggerated quadratically. On ImageNet with pixels in \([0,1]\), \(\lambda\approx(0.2175,\,0.0188,\,0.0045)\) and \(p_1\approx-(0.57,0.58,0.58)\), which is the grey axis. Fancy PCA is therefore almost entirely a brightness jitter. That fits Table 1, where it scores 49.4% against colour jittering's 49.6%. The second panel of Try It lets you see this.

Tiny example

Toy covariance with red and green correlated: \(\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&0\\1&2&0\\0&0&0.5\end{pmatrix}\). Its eigenpairs are \(\lambda_1=3,\ p_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1,0)\); \(\lambda_2=1,\ p_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1,0)\); \(\lambda_3=0.5,\ p_3=(0,0,1)\).

Draw \(\alpha=(0.1,-0.2,0.4)\). The shift is \(0.3\,p_1-0.2\,p_2+0.2\,p_3\approx(0.071,\ 0.354,\ 0.200)\), added to every pixel: the whole image tilts green-blue.

Across draws, the shift's standard deviation along \(p_1\) versus \(p_3\) is \(0.1\cdot3 : 0.1\cdot0.5 = 6:1\). The data's own ratio is only \(\sqrt{3/0.5}\approx2.45:1\).

IV · Cool Things

FindingsFrom the survey, and one classic

Style is a Gram matrix, which forgets where\(FF^\top\)

Neural style transfer, which the survey uses to repaint training images in new textures, measures “style” at a CNN layer with \(F\in\mathbb R^{C\times N}\): \(C\) feature channels at \(N\) spatial positions. Style is the Gram matrix \(G=FF^\top=\sum_{\text{positions}}f_nf_n^\top\), which is \(C\times C\), symmetric and PSD. For any permutation \(Q\) of positions, \((FQ)(FQ)^\top=FF^\top\). So \(G\) records which features occur together and discards where they occur: texture without layout. Example: \(F=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&2\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}\) and the same \(F\) with columns 1 and 3 swapped both give \(G=\begin{pmatrix}5&2\\2&2\end{pmatrix}\).

Adversarial noise is a dual-norm fact\(\varepsilon\|g\|_1\)

The fast gradient sign method perturbs an image by \(\delta=\varepsilon\,\operatorname{sign}(g)\), where \(g=\nabla_xJ\) is the gradient of the classifier's loss \(J\) with respect to the pixels. To first order this is optimal: \(\max_{\|\delta\|_\infty\le\varepsilon}g^\top\delta=\varepsilon\|g\|_1\), because \(\ell_1\) is the dual norm of \(\ell_\infty\). Each pixel moves by at most \(\varepsilon\) (0.007 in the survey's panda-to-gibbon figure), but \(\|g\|_1\) grows with the number of pixels. That explains the survey's remark that attacks get stronger as resolution rises. A network fooled on 89.4% of such MNIST (handwritten-digit) images at 97.6% confidence fell to 17.9% after training on them.

Noise injection is ridge regression in disguise\(\sigma^2\|w\|^2\)

This one isn't in the survey; it is a classic result (Bishop, 1995). For a linear model with weights \(w\) and noise \(\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2I)\) added to the inputs, \(\mathbb E\,(y-w^\top(x+\varepsilon))^2=(y-w^\top x)^2+\sigma^2\|w\|^2\). Training on noisy copies is exactly training with an \(\ell_2\) penalty. Augmentation and regularisation are the same thing seen from two sides.

Experts can't spot the fakes62.5%, 58.6%

Two radiologists shown a mix of real liver-lesion images and lesions generated by a GAN correctly flagged only 62.5% and 58.6% of the fakes. That is close to the 50% of guessing, and it is why GAN samples work as extra training data in medicine.

V · If You Remember One Thing

The one thing to remember

Most image augmentations are simple structured matrices acting on the pixel vector: permutations, selections, convolutions, a \(3\times3\) colour map. Whether one helps depends on whether the label is really invariant under that matrix, not on how much information it preserves. The information-destroying crop beat every orthogonal flip.
