Find the Box

Object Detection with Deep Learning: A Review

Zhong-Qiu Zhao, Peng Zheng, Shou-tao Xu et al. · 2018 · arXiv:1807.05511

A survey of how convolutional neural networks learned to find every object in a photo, draw a rectangle around it and name it. It follows the line from R-CNN (2014), which ran a full network on 2,000 crops per image, to single-pass detectors that run on live video, and then looks at faces, pedestrians and “salient” objects.

I · In 60 Seconds

Problemwhere + what

Object detection means: given a photo, output a list of rectangles (“bounding boxes”), each with a class label such as dog or car and a confidence. That is two problems at once: classification (what) and localisation (where), for an unknown number of objects of any size.

Before 2012 the recipe was: slide windows of many sizes over the image, describe each window with hand-designed features (for instance histograms of edge directions), and score it with a linear classifier. Progress on the standard benchmark, PASCAL VOC (20 everyday classes), had stalled around 2010–2012.

Idealearn the features

Use a convolutional neural network (CNN), a network built from small sliding filters that is pretrained on 1.2 million labelled ImageNet photos, as the feature extractor. Two families came out of this.

Two-stage (R-CNN and its successors): first propose about 2,000 candidate boxes, then classify each and nudge its coordinates. One-stage (YOLO, SSD): a single network predicts boxes and classes over a fixed grid in one pass. Each later model mostly removes some piece of repeated work from the one before it.

Result33 s → 0.02 s

Detectors are scored by mAP (mean average precision): for each class, rank all predicted boxes by confidence and take the area under the precision–recall curve, counting a box as correct if its overlap with a true box is at least 0.5 (overlap = area of intersection ÷ area of union, called IoU); then average over classes.

On PASCAL VOC 2007, R-CNN scores 66.0 mAP at 32.8 seconds per image. Faster R-CNN reaches 73.2 at 0.11 s. YOLO runs at 45 frames per second (63.4 mAP); SSD at 300×300 pixels manages 46 fps at 74.3. On the harder COCO benchmark (80 classes, many small objects), the best entry in the review is Mask R-CNN with 39.8 AP, against 20.5 for Fast R-CNN. The survey's verdict: two-stage detectors are still more accurate, one-stage ones much faster.

II · The Linear Algebra

MachineryShift-commuting operators

The review is a survey, so it has no theorem of its own. Underneath, though, the whole R-CNN → Fast → Faster story rests on one structural fact about the convolution operator, plus two smaller tools: a low-rank factorisation and a careful choice of coordinates for boxes.

The objects

\(F\in\mathbb R^{H\times W\times C}\)feature map

What a CNN layer outputs: a coarse \(H\times W\) grid of positions with a \(C\)-dimensional feature vector at each one (for example \(C=256\) channels after the fifth convolution layer in the network SPP-net uses). The input image is the case \(C=3\) (red, green, blue).

\(K\)sparse, Toeplitz

A convolution layer. Flatten a one-channel map to \(x\in\mathbb R^{n}\), \(n=HW\). A \(3\times3\) filter is a linear map \(K:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^n\) whose every row has at most 9 nonzeros, and every row holds the same 9 numbers, shifted. It is doubly block Toeplitz (circulant if the image wraps around at its edges). With channels, \(K\) is a \(C_{\text{out}}\times C_{\text{in}}\) grid of such blocks: \(9\,C_{\text{in}}C_{\text{out}}\) parameters, regardless of image size. A pointwise nonlinearity (ReLU, \(t\mapsto\max(t,0)\)) follows.

\(I_{HW}\otimes M\)1×1 convolution

A \(1\times1\) convolution applies one \(C_{\text{out}}\times C_{\text{in}}\) matrix \(M\) at every position, so it is block diagonal: a Kronecker product with the identity. Faster R-CNN's proposal heads are exactly this: at each position, \(M\) maps a 512-dimensional feature vector to \(2k\) object/not-object scores and \(4k\) box coordinates for \(k=9\) reference boxes.

\(P_R\)0/1 selection

Cropping to a rectangular region \(R\): the rows of the identity indexed by the pixels of \(R\). Every detector has to apply something like \(P_R\) roughly 2,000 times per image.

The fact that does the work: convolution commutes with shifts

Let \(S\) be the operator that translates an image by one pixel. Because every row of \(K\) holds the same weights,

$$KS=SK \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad P_R\,K\,x \;=\; K_R\,P_R\,x\ \ \text{except within the kernel's radius of } \partial R,$$

where \(K_R\) is the same filter run on the crop alone and \(\partial R\) is the region's border. In words: the features of a crop are a crop of the features. Stacking layers preserves this, since pointwise ReLU commutes with \(S\) too (max-pooling with stride \(s\) commutes with shifts by multiples of \(s\)). The band where the two disagree just widens to the network's receptive field.

Spectrally: with wrap-around boundaries \(K\) is circulant, so every such \(K\) is diagonalised by the same discrete Fourier basis, with eigenvalues the Fourier transform of the filter. Shift-commuting operators are precisely these simultaneously diagonalisable ones.

This is the history in one equation. R-CNN computes \(K_R P_R x\): it crops each of ~2,000 proposals, resizes to 227×227, and runs the full CNN on each, 32.8 s per image. SPP-net and Fast R-CNN compute \(Kx\) once for the whole image and then crop the feature map, \(P_R(Kx)\). The difference is a thin border band, and it cut test time to 2.3 s and 1.7 s. Faster R-CNN then generates the proposals themselves from the same \(Kx\) with \(1\times1\) heads: 0.11 s.

Tiny example: a 4-pixel circular image

Filter: \(y_i = x_{i-1} + 2x_i - x_{i+1}\), indices mod 4. As a matrix, and on a one-hot input:

$$K=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&0&1\\1&2&-1&0\\0&1&2&-1\\-1&0&1&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad K\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\2\\1\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad K\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\-1\\2\\1\end{pmatrix}.$$

Shift the input down one slot and the output shifts down one slot: \(KS=SK\). Each row is the previous one rotated right, i.e. \(K\) is circulant. Its eigenvectors are \(v_k=(1,\omega^k,\omega^{2k},\omega^{3k})\), \(\omega=e^{2\pi i/4}\), with eigenvalues \(\lambda_k = 2+\omega^{-k}-\omega^{k} = 2-2i\sin(\pi k/2)\), i.e. \(2,\ 2-2i,\ 2,\ 2+2i\). \(K\) is not symmetric, but it is normal.

From a crop of any size to a fixed-length vector. A cropped feature map has a different shape for every region, but the next layer is an ordinary matrix with fixed input size. RoI pooling (RoI = region of interest) splits the region into a fixed \(7\times7\) grid of bins and takes the max in each bin, per channel. SPP-net uses several grids at once (1×1, 2×2, 4×4), giving \(256\times(1+4+16)=5376\) numbers for any region. Mask R-CNN later replaced the hard rounding to bin boundaries with bilinear interpolation (RoIAlign), because rounding breaks pixel-exact alignment.

Tool 2: truncated SVD on the fully connected layers

After pooling, each region passes through fully connected layers \(y=Wx\), \(W\in\mathbb R^{u\times v}\). These run once per region, so in Fast R-CNN they dominate test time. The fix the review cites is the truncated singular value decomposition, keeping the top \(t\) singular triples:

$$W\approx U_t\,\Sigma_t\,V_t^{\top},\qquad Wx\approx U_t\big(\Sigma_tV_t^{\top}x\big),\qquad uv\ \text{ multiply-adds}\ \to\ t(u+v).$$

One dense layer becomes two thin ones (\(t\times v\), then \(u\times t\)), with no nonlinearity between them. By Eckart–Young this is the best rank-\(t\) replacement in both the spectral and Frobenius norms. For VGG16's first such layer, \(7\cdot7\cdot512=25{,}088\) inputs to 4,096 outputs (about 103M weights), \(t=1024\) leaves about 30M.

Tool 3: coordinates for boxes, and why the logs

A detector never regresses raw pixel coordinates. A box is centre \((x,y)\), width \(w\), height \(h\). Each prediction is made relative to a reference box \(a=(x_a,y_a,w_a,h_a)\): a proposal in R-CNN, or an anchor, one of 9 fixed boxes (3 sizes × 3 aspect ratios) tiled at every grid position, in Faster R-CNN and SSD. The network outputs

$$t_x=\frac{x-x_a}{w_a},\quad t_y=\frac{y-y_a}{h_a},\quad t_w=\log\frac{w}{w_a},\quad t_h=\log\frac{h}{h_a}.$$

Think of a box as the affine map \(g(u)=\operatorname{diag}(w,h)\,u+(x,y)\) that places the unit square. These maps form a group (axis-aligned scaling plus translation). Then

$$g_a^{-1}g\,(u)=\operatorname{diag}\!\Big(\tfrac{w}{w_a},\tfrac{h}{h_a}\Big)u+\Big(\tfrac{x-x_a}{w_a},\tfrac{y-y_a}{h_a}\Big),$$

so \(t\) is just the translation part of \(g_a^{-1}g\) and the log of its scale part. If you rescale and move the whole scene by any \(h\) in the group, \((hg_a)^{-1}(hg)=g_a^{-1}g\): the target \(t\) is unchanged. The log turns the multiplicative scale group into the additive reals, so “twice as wide” and “half as wide” are \(\pm0.69\), symmetric about 0.

Tiny example

Anchor: centre \((100,100)\), \(64\times64\). True box: centre \((116,92)\), \(128\times48\). Then \(t=(16/64,\,-8/64,\,\log 2,\,\log 0.75)=(0.25,\,-0.125,\,0.693,\,-0.288)\).

Photograph the same scene at twice the resolution: anchor \((200,200)\), \(128\times128\); box \((232,184)\), \(256\times96\). Pixel errors double, \(t\) is identical. One regressor serves every scale.

The loss (Fast R-CNN, eq. 1–3 in the review) adds the two tasks. Let \(p=(p_0,\dots,p_C)\) be the predicted probabilities over \(C\) classes plus background (class 0), \(u\) the true class, \(v\) the true box offsets and \(t^u\) the predicted ones for class \(u\):

$$L=-\log p_u+\lambda\,[u\ge1]\sum_{i\in\{x,y,w,h\}}\operatorname{smooth}_{L_1}(t^u_i-v_i),\qquad \operatorname{smooth}_{L_1}(z)=\begin{cases}\tfrac12 z^2,&\lvert z\rvert\lt 1\\ \lvert z\rvert-\tfrac12,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

The bracket \([u\ge1]\) is 1 for real objects and 0 for background, which has no box to fit. Smooth \(L_1\) is the Huber loss: its derivative is \(z\) clipped to \([-1,1]\), so one badly wrong proposal cannot produce an exploding gradient.

IV · Cool Things

FindingsFrom the review's tables

300× faster by reordering two operators32.8 s → 0.11 s

Table V: R-CNN takes 32.84 s per image at 66.0 mAP; Faster R-CNN with the same VGG16 backbone takes 0.11 s at 73.2. Faster and more accurate. The speed comes from sharing \(Kx\) and from replacing the 2-second CPU proposal step with \(1\times1\) heads. The accuracy comes largely from training the whole pipeline with one loss through every layer, instead of R-CNN's separately trained stages.

Detection needs to break the symmetry it exploitsR-FCN, \(k^2\) maps

Classification wants invariance: a cat shifted left is still a cat. Detection needs variance: a box shifted left is a worse box. A purely convolutional detector, all \(KS=SK\), turned out worse. R-FCN fixes this by making \(k^2\) score maps per class, one per cell of a \(k\times k\) grid on the region (“top-left part of a cat”, “centre of a cat”, …). Each region cell reads only its own map. Position enters through which map is read, and all computation is still shared. With a ResNet-101 backbone and extra COCO training data, it reaches 85.0 mAP on VOC 2012, the best in the review's table, at 0.17 s per image.

YOLO's square roots\(\sqrt w\)

YOLO regresses \(\sqrt{w}\) and \(\sqrt{h}\) instead of \(w,h\) with a squared loss. Since \(d\sqrt w = dw/(2\sqrt w)\), a 10-pixel width error costs about 4× more on a box of width 25 than on one of width 400 in \(\sqrt{\cdot}\) units. It is a cheap stand-in for the scale-free \(\log\) of the anchor coordinates above, and YOLO's weak spot was in fact small objects and precise localisation.

Pedestrians held out longest17.1 vs 8.2

On the Caltech pedestrian benchmark (miss rate, lower is better), the hand-crafted Checkerboards+ scores 17.1 and an early CNN (SCF+AlexNet) a worse 23.3. The best CNN system, F-DNN+SS, gets 8.2. Many pedestrians are only a few dozen pixels tall, and RoI pooling over such a tiny region collapses its bins into near-identical values, so generic detectors needed real redesign there.

V · If You Remember One Thing

The one thing to remember

Because convolution commutes with translation, the features of a crop are (up to a thin border) a crop of the whole image's features. Computing them once and sharing them across thousands of candidate boxes is what took deep object detection from 33 seconds per image to real time.
