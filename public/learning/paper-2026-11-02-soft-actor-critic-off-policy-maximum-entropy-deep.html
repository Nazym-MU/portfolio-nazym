Soft Actor-Critic

Off-Policy Maximum Entropy Deep Reinforcement Learning with a Stochastic Actor

Tuomas Haarnoja, Aurick Zhou, Pieter Abbeel et al. · 2018 · arXiv:1801.01290

A learning agent that controls a simulated robot is rewarded for reaching its goal and, in this paper, also for acting as randomly as it can while still reaching it. That one change makes a learning method that reuses old experience both stable and fast, and it beat every method the authors compared it with on the hardest robot-control tasks they tried.

I · In 60 Seconds

Problemdata-hungry or brittle

In reinforcement learning, an agent repeatedly observes a state (here, a simulated robot's joint angles and velocities), picks an action (the torques for each joint), and receives a number called the reward. Its rule for picking actions is the policy, and the goal is a policy with a large total reward over time.

The two families of methods in 2018 each had a flaw. On-policy methods, such as PPO, learn only from data gathered with the current policy and throw it away after each update, so they need millions of trial steps. Off-policy methods, such as DDPG, keep every past step in a replay buffer and reuse it, which is far cheaper, but they were notoriously unstable: change a learning rate or a random seed and training could fail.

Ideareward + α · entropy

Reward the agent for entropy too: the randomness of its action distribution. The agent maximises reward plus \(\alpha\) times entropy, where \(\alpha\) is a fixed weight called the temperature. The policy then spreads its choices over every action that is nearly as good as the best one, instead of collapsing onto one.

The algorithm is an actor-critic. The critic is a neural network that estimates how good each action is in each state. The actor is a second network, the policy itself: for each state it outputs a Gaussian distribution over actions. Both learn from the replay buffer, so the method is off-policy.

Result≈6,000 vs ≈2,000

The tests are simulated robots that must learn to walk or run, from a one-legged hopper (3 joint torques to choose) to a humanoid (21). On the hardest, the 21-joint humanoid, soft actor-critic reaches an average score of about 6,000 within 10 million steps. PPO and the earlier entropy-based method reach about 2,000, and DDPG makes no progress at all (values read off the paper's Figure 1).

Its five runs with different random seeds give nearly the same curve. Only one setting, the scale of the reward, had to be tuned per task.

II · The Linear Algebra

MachineryContraction, softmax, inverse curvature

The setting is a Markov decision process. At time \(t\) the agent is in state \(s_t\), takes action \(a_t\), receives reward \(r(s_t,a_t)\), and the world moves to \(s_{t+1}\) with probability \(p(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)\). The policy \(\pi(a\mid s)\) is a probability distribution over actions for each state. A discount \(\gamma\in[0,1)\) (0.99 in the paper) shrinks rewards that arrive later. The paper's objective, with \(\mathcal H\) the entropy of the policy's distribution at the states the agent visits, is

$$J(\pi)=\sum_t \mathbb E\big[\,r(s_t,a_t)+\alpha\,\mathcal H(\pi(\cdot\mid s_t))\,\big],\qquad \mathcal H(q)=-\mathbb E_{a\sim q}\log q(a).$$

Setting \(\alpha=0\) gives ordinary reinforcement learning. Scaling the reward by \(1/\alpha\) gives the same optimum with \(\alpha=1\), so from here on take \(\alpha=1\) unless it is shown.

The paper's theory runs on finitely many states and actions. There, everything is a vector or a matrix, and the argument is three facts of linear algebra.

The objects, in the finite case

\(Q\in\mathbb R^{|S||A|}\)the critic

The soft Q-function: one number per state–action pair, the expected future reward plus entropy bonus from taking \(a\) in \(s\) and then following \(\pi\). \(|S|\) and \(|A|\) are the numbers of states and actions.

\(P\in\mathbb R^{|S||A|\times|S|}\)the world

Row \((s,a)\) is the distribution \(p(\cdot\mid s,a)\) of next states. Entrywise nonnegative, each row sums to 1 (row-stochastic). Unknown to the agent.

\(M_\pi\in\mathbb R^{|S|\times|S||A|}\)the policy

Row \(s\) holds \(\pi(\cdot\mid s)\) in the columns belonging to state \(s\) and zeros elsewhere. Also row-stochastic, and block-sparse.

\(P^\pi=P\,M_\pi\)rank ≤ |S|

The transition matrix between state–action pairs: \(P^\pi_{(s,a),(s',a')}=p(s'\mid s,a)\,\pi(a'\mid s')\). A product of row-stochastic matrices is row-stochastic. It factors through the much smaller state space, so on \(\mathbb R^{|S||A|}\) it has rank at most \(|S|\).

Fact 1 · Evaluating a policy is an affine contraction

The paper's soft Bellman backup (its Eqs. 2–3) updates \(Q\) to “reward now, plus the discounted soft value of where you land”. Written with the matrices above, it is an affine map:

$$\mathcal T^\pi Q \;=\; r_\pi+\gamma\,P^\pi Q,\qquad r_\pi(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\,\mathbb E_{s'\sim p(\cdot\mid s,a)}\,\mathcal H\big(\pi(\cdot\mid s')\big).$$

The vector \(r_\pi\) is the reward with the expected entropy of the next state's action distribution folded in. Lemma 1 says iterating \(Q_{k+1}=\mathcal T^\pi Q_k\) from any start converges to the soft Q-function \(Q^\pi\) of the policy.

The spectral fact doing the work

A row-stochastic matrix has \(\|P^\pi\|_{\infty}=1\) (the max-row-sum norm), so every eigenvalue satisfies \(|\lambda|\le1\), and \(\mathbf 1\) is an eigenvector with \(\lambda=1\). Hence

$$\|\mathcal T^\pi Q-\mathcal T^\pi Q'\|_\infty=\gamma\|P^\pi(Q-Q')\|_\infty\le\gamma\|Q-Q'\|_\infty,$$ $$Q^\pi=(I-\gamma P^\pi)^{-1}r_\pi=\sum_{k\ge0}\gamma^k(P^\pi)^k r_\pi .$$

The spectral radius of \(\gamma P^\pi\) is \(\gamma<1\), so the Neumann series converges, \(I-\gamma P^\pi\) is invertible, and the error shrinks by at least \(\gamma\) per sweep. The paper needs finitely many actions only so that the entropy bonus, at most \(\log|A|\), keeps \(r_\pi\) bounded.

Tiny example

Two state–action pairs that always lead to each other: \(P^\pi=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\), with \(r_\pi=\binom{1}{0}\) and \(\gamma=0.5\).

\(I-\gamma P^\pi=\begin{pmatrix}1&-0.5\\-0.5&1\end{pmatrix}\) has determinant \(0.75\), so \(Q^\pi=\tfrac{1}{0.75}\begin{pmatrix}1&0.5\\0.5&1\end{pmatrix}\binom10=\binom{4/3}{2/3}\). Check: \(4/3=1+0.5\cdot\tfrac23\) and \(2/3=0+0.5\cdot\tfrac43\).

The eigenvalues of \(\gamma P^\pi\) are \(\pm0.5\), so starting from \(Q_0=0\) the error halves every sweep.

Fact 2 · Improving a policy is a softmax

Given the current critic \(Q^{\pi_{\text{old}}}\), the new policy at each state is the distribution closest to the softmax of \(Q\), the paper's Eq. 4. Closeness is measured by the KL divergence \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\Vert p)=\mathbb E_q[\log q-\log p]\), and \(\Pi\) is the family of policies we allow, such as Gaussians:

$$\pi_{\text{new}}(\cdot\mid s)=\arg\min_{q\in\Pi}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\Big(q\;\Big\Vert\;\frac{e^{Q(s,\cdot)}}{Z(s)}\Big),\qquad Z(s)=\sum_a e^{Q(s,a)}.$$

Why the softmax: expand the KL and you get, for any distribution \(q\),

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q\,\Vert\,e^{Q}/Z\big)=\log Z-\big(\mathbb E_q[Q]+\mathcal H(q)\big).$$

The bracket is exactly \(q\)'s soft value under the current critic. So minimising KL is maximising soft value, and since KL \(\ge0\), the best possible is \(\log Z\) (the convex-duality fact that log-sum-exp is the conjugate of negative entropy). The normaliser \(Z\) doesn't depend on \(q\), which is why the algorithm never needs to compute it.

Tiny example

One state, two actions, \(Q=(1,0)\). The softmax is \(\pi=(\tfrac{e}{1+e},\tfrac{1}{1+e})=(0.731,\,0.269)\): \(\mathbb E_\pi Q=0.731\), \(\mathcal H(\pi)=0.582\), total \(1.313=\log(1+e)=\log Z\).

Always taking the better action scores \(1+0=1\). Uniform scores \(0.5+\log2=1.193\). Both lose to the softmax.

Why the new policy is better everywhere (Lemma 2). Since \(\pi_{\text{old}}\in\Pi\) was a candidate, the KL-minimiser's soft value under \(Q^{\pi_{\text{old}}}\) is at least the old one, state by state: call the gap \(\delta(s)\ge0\). Subtract the two soft Bellman equations and the difference \(D=Q^{\pi_{\text{new}}}-Q^{\pi_{\text{old}}}\) satisfies \(D=\gamma P^{\pi_{\text{new}}}D+\gamma P\delta\), so

$$D=(I-\gamma P^{\pi_{\text{new}}})^{-1}\gamma P\,\delta=\sum_{k\ge0}\gamma^{k}(P^{\pi_{\text{new}}})^k\,\gamma P\,\delta\;\ge\;0\ \text{ entrywise}.$$

The Neumann series of a nonnegative matrix is nonnegative, so \((I-\gamma P^\pi)^{-1}\) preserves the order “\(\ge\) in every entry”. A gain at each state becomes a gain at every state–action pair. Theorem 1 then follows: the \(Q\)'s rise monotonically, are bounded, and converge to the best policy in \(\Pi\).

Fact 3 · With continuous actions, the policy's covariance is the critic's inverse curvature

For a robot, the action is a vector \(a\in\mathbb R^n\) of \(n\) joint torques, and \(\Pi\) is the Gaussians \(\mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma)\) with mean \(\mu\) and covariance \(\Sigma\) produced by the actor network. Near its peak, write the critic as a quadratic in the action: peak value \(q_0\) at action \(m\), and \(C\) the symmetric positive-definite matrix of its curvature (minus its Hessian):

$$Q(s,a)\approx q_0-\tfrac12(a-m)^{\top}C\,(a-m)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{e^{Q/\alpha}}{Z}=\mathcal N\big(m,\;\alpha\,C^{-1}\big).$$

So the softmax target is itself Gaussian, the projection is exact, and the policy's covariance is the temperature times the inverse curvature. Diagonalise \(C=R\,\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)R^{\top}\): along the eigenvector \(u_i\) the policy's standard deviation is \(\sqrt{\alpha/\lambda_i}\). The agent explores most in the directions where the critic is flattest, where trying something different costs least.

Restricted to a diagonal covariance

The usual choice (and the released code's) is a diagonal \(\Sigma\), which can't tilt. Projecting \(\mathcal N(m,\alpha C^{-1})\) onto diagonal Gaussians with the paper's KL gives \(\sigma_i^2=\alpha/C_{ii}\) (set \(\partial/\partial\sigma_i^2\) of \(\tfrac12[\operatorname{tr}(C\Sigma)/\alpha-\log\det\Sigma]\) to zero). The KL lost is

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}=\tfrac12\log\frac{\prod_i C_{ii}}{\det C}\;\ge\;0\quad\text{(Hadamard's inequality)},$$

zero iff \(C\) is diagonal, and independent of \(\alpha\).

Tiny example. \(\alpha=1\), \(C=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\), with eigenvalues 3 along \((1,1)\) and 1 along \((1,-1)\). The exact policy is \(C^{-1}=\tfrac13\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}\): variance \(1\) along \((1,-1)\), \(1/3\) along \((1,1)\). The diagonal policy has \(\sigma_i^2=1/2\), smaller than the true marginal \(2/3\). It under-explores, by \(\tfrac12\log\tfrac43\approx0.14\) nats.

From theory to the algorithm

Exact iteration is impossible for continuous states, so each piece becomes a network trained by stochastic gradient descent on batches from the replay buffer. Critic: \(Q_\theta\) is fitted to the target \(r+\gamma V_{\bar\psi}(s')\), where \(V\) is a separate state-value network, and \(\bar\psi\) is a slowly moving copy of its weights, \(\bar\psi\leftarrow0.005\,\psi+0.995\,\bar\psi\). Actor: minimise the KL above, which with \(Z\) dropped is (Eq. 12)

$$J_\pi(\phi)=\mathbb E_{s,\ \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)}\Big[\log\pi_\phi(a\mid s)-Q_\theta(s,a)\Big],\qquad a=\mu_\phi(s)+\Sigma_\phi(s)^{1/2}\varepsilon .$$

Writing the sampled action as an affine function of fixed noise \(\varepsilon\) (the reparameterisation trick) lets the gradient flow through \(\nabla_aQ\), the critic's slope in action space. The alternative estimator, which only weights \(\nabla\log\pi\) by sampled values, has much higher variance.

IV · Cool Things

FindingsFrom the paper

The only knob is a temperaturereward scale 5–20

Multiplying rewards by a constant is the same as dividing \(\alpha\) by it. This was the only setting tuned per task: 5 for the small robots, 10 or 20 for the humanoids. Too small, and the policy stays nearly uniform and ignores the reward. Too large, and it turns nearly deterministic early and stalls for lack of exploration. Every other setting (2 hidden layers of 256 units, learning rate \(3\cdot10^{-4}\)) was shared by all tasks.

Squashing costs only a diagonal Jacobianlog det = a sum

Torques must lie in \((-1,1)\), so the Gaussian sample \(u\) is passed through \(a=\tanh(u)\) entrywise. Change of variables needs \(\log|\det\partial a/\partial u|\). That Jacobian is \(\mathrm{diag}(1-\tanh^2u_i)\), so the log-determinant is \(\sum_i\log(1-\tanh^2u_i)\): \(O(n)\) instead of \(O(n^3)\), even for the 21-dimensional humanoid.

Randomness buys stability5 seeds

The authors also ran a version whose policy is deterministic and has no entropy term, otherwise much like SAC. On the 21-joint humanoid, its five seeded runs scatter wildly, while SAC's five almost coincide. At test time, though, SAC does better if it just plays the Gaussian's mean: the randomness is for learning, not for performing.

Two critics, take the min\(\mathbb E\max\ge\max\mathbb E\)

An actor that climbs a noisy critic seeks out the critic's errors upward. By Jensen's inequality, the maximum of a noisy estimate is biased high. SAC trains two independent critics and uses the smaller of the two, which counteracts the bias. The authors found this markedly sped up learning on the harder tasks.

V · If You Remember One Thing

The one thing to remember

Rewarding entropy turns policy improvement into a softmax of the critic, and for continuous actions that softmax is a Gaussian with covariance \(\alpha C^{-1}\), where \(C\) is the critic's curvature. So the agent explores most where mistakes are cheapest, and that is what keeps off-policy learning stable.
