I · In 60 Seconds

Problemdata or equations, rarely both

Physics is usually simulated by solving a partial differential equation (PDE): an equation linking a field, such as temperature \(u(x,t)\) at position \(x\) and time \(t\), to its rates of change in space and time. Classical solvers chop the domain into a mesh and solve a huge linear system. They are excellent when the equation, coefficients and boundary values are all known. They are awkward when part of the information comes as scattered, noisy measurements, when the geometry is hard to mesh, when there are many dimensions, or for inverse problems: recovering an unknown coefficient or source from observations of the solution.

Plain machine learning goes the other way: it fits data but needs a lot of it, and it knows nothing about conservation laws. Scientific data is usually scarce.

Ideathe equation is training data

Let a neural network \(u_\theta(x,t)\), with weights \(\theta\), stand for the unknown field. Automatic differentiation gives its exact derivatives, so at thousands of randomly scattered collocation points you can measure how badly it violates the PDE. Train on a sum of three penalties: misfit to measurements, PDE violation at the collocation points, and violation of the boundary and initial conditions. This is a physics-informed neural network (PINN). No mesh is needed, and an unknown coefficient in the equation can simply become one more trainable number.

The review sorts ways of putting physics into a model into three kinds of bias: observational (data that already carries the physics, such as simulations or symmetry-augmented samples), inductive (architectures that satisfy a law exactly, such as built-in conservation or symmetry), and learning (penalties in the loss, as in PINNs).

Resulta review, not a benchmark

There is no single headline number. The review's case is that one formulation handles forward problems (solve the PDE), inverse problems (find hidden parameters or missing physics) and high-dimensional problems, and that it degrades gracefully with sparse, noisy data. It is equally clear about limits. Training is slow and often fails on stiff, multiscale or high-frequency solutions. For a well-posed forward problem a classical solver is usually still faster and more accurate. The theory is immature. The next section shows the linear algebra behind the training difficulty.

II · The Linear Algebra

MachineryA Gram matrix and an operator's spectrum

The setup

Write the PDE as \(\mathcal N[u]=f\) inside a domain \(\Omega\) and \(\mathcal B[u]=g\) on its boundary \(\partial\Omega\). Here \(\mathcal N\) is a differential operator (for heat flow, \(\mathcal N[u]=u_t-\nu u_{xx}\), with \(\nu\) the diffusivity), \(f\) is a known source, and \(\mathcal B\) says what happens at the edge (for example \(\mathcal B[u]=u\), fixing the value). You also have \(N_d\) measurements \(u_i\) at points \(x_i\). The PINN loss is

$$\mathcal L(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N_d}\big(u_\theta(x_i)-u_i\big)^2+\frac{w_r}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N_r}\big(\mathcal N[u_\theta](x_j)-f(x_j)\big)^2+\frac{w_b}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_b}\big(\mathcal B[u_\theta](x_\ell)-g(x_\ell)\big)^2 ,$$

where the \(x_j\) are \(N_r\) collocation points inside \(\Omega\), the \(x_\ell\) are \(N_b\) points on the boundary, and \(w_r, w_b\) are weights you choose.

The objects

\(r(\theta)\in\mathbb R^{N}\)\(N=N_d+N_r+N_b\)

All residuals stacked: data misfits, then PDE residuals, then boundary residuals (each scaled by the square root of its weight). The loss is just \(\tfrac12\|r\|^2\).

\(J=\partial r/\partial\theta\in\mathbb R^{N\times P}\)dense, tall or wide

The Jacobian, with \(P\) the number of weights. Row \(j\) is the gradient of residual \(j\) with respect to the weights. For a PDE row with linear \(\mathcal N\), that row is \(\mathcal N\) applied to the network's gradient: \(\nabla_\theta\,\mathcal N[u_\theta](x_j)=\mathcal N\big[\nabla_\theta u_\theta\big](x_j)\).

\(K=JJ^{\top}\in\mathbb R^{N\times N}\)symmetric PSD

The Gram matrix of those gradients, called the neural tangent kernel (NTK) for networks. Entry \(K_{jj'}\) is how much a step that reduces residual \(j\) also moves residual \(j'\). It inherits the block structure of \(r\), and for linear \(\mathcal N\) the PDE block is the data kernel with \(\mathcal N\) applied in both arguments: \(K_{rr}(x,x')=\mathcal N_x\mathcal N_{x'}K_{uu}(x,x')\).

Train by gradient flow, \(\dot\theta=-\nabla\mathcal L=-J^{\top}r\). By the chain rule \(\dot r=J\dot\theta\), so

$$\dot r=-K\,r,\qquad K=Q\Lambda Q^{\top}\ \Longrightarrow\ r(t)=\sum_i e^{-\lambda_i t}\,\big(q_i^{\top}r(0)\big)\,q_i .$$

For a linear model \(K\) is constant and this is exact; for very wide networks it holds approximately. Each eigenvector \(q_i\) of \(K\) is a pattern of residuals, and it dies at rate \(\lambda_i\). With discrete steps of size \(\eta\), stability needs \(\eta<2/\lambda_{\max}\), so the slowest pattern takes on the order of \(\kappa=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\) steps. The condition number of \(K\) sets the training time. (\(J^{\top}J\), the Gauss–Newton approximation to the Hessian in weight space, has the same nonzero eigenvalues.)

The spectral fact that does the work: a differential operator scales mode k by k² and the loss squares it

Take the simplest PDE: \(u''=f\) on \((0,1)\) with \(u(0)=u(1)=0\). With these boundary conditions, \(\tfrac{d^2}{dx^2}\) is a symmetric (self-adjoint) operator with eigenfunctions and eigenvalues

$$\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\sin(k\pi x)=-(k\pi)^2\sin(k\pi x),\qquad k=1,2,\dots$$

Use these as features: \(u_\theta=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\theta_k\sin(k\pi x)\), and let \(\theta^\star\) be the true coefficients. Using \(\int_0^1\sin^2(k\pi x)\,dx=\tfrac12\) and orthogonality, the continuous physics loss diagonalises:

$$\tfrac12\!\int_0^1\!\big(u_\theta''-f\big)^2dx=\tfrac12(\theta-\theta^\star)^{\top}H(\theta-\theta^\star),\qquad H=\operatorname{diag}\!\Big(\tfrac{(k\pi)^4}{2}\Big)_{k=1}^{m},\qquad \kappa(H)=m^4 .$$

Fitting \(u\) directly to data gives \(H=\tfrac12 I\) with \(\kappa=1\). Going through the operator multiplies mode \(k\) by \(k^2\), and the squared loss turns that into \(k^4\). So the PINN loss is as ill-conditioned as \(\mathcal N^*\mathcal N\), with \(\mathcal N^*\) the adjoint, and a second-order operator makes that bad fast.

Tiny example

Two modes, \(m=2\): \(H=\operatorname{diag}(\pi^4/2,\ 8\pi^4)\approx\operatorname{diag}(48.7,\ 779.3)\), so \(\kappa=16\).

Gradient descent on error \(e=\theta-\theta^\star\) gives \(e\mapsto(I-\eta H)e\). The largest stable-and-fastest choice is \(\eta=1/779.3\). Then mode 2's error is wiped out in one step, but mode 1's shrinks only by a factor \(1-48.7/779.3=0.9375\) per step. Getting it below 1% takes \(\lceil\ln 0.01/\ln 0.9375\rceil=72\) steps.

With \(m=8\) modes, \(\kappa=8^4=4096\) and the same target takes about 18,900 steps. The smoothest part of the solution, usually the part you care about most, is learned last.

The same arithmetic explains the other well-known PINN pathology: imbalance between blocks. The PDE block \(K_{rr}\) carries those \(k^4\) factors and the boundary block \(K_{bb}\) does not, so \(\operatorname{tr}K_{rr}\gg\operatorname{tr}K_{bb}\). Gradient descent then satisfies the equation in the interior long before it respects the boundary conditions, and without them the solution is not unique. A common remedy in this line of work is to set the weights so the blocks have comparable traces, e.g. \(w_b\propto\operatorname{tr}K_{rr}/\operatorname{tr}K_{bb}\). Rescaling a block is a crude diagonal preconditioner of \(K\).

III · Try It: Why the Physics Loss Is Slow

InteractiveGradient descent, mode by mode

The setting is the callout's toy: \(u''=f\) on \((0,1)\), zero at both ends, with the eight sine modes \(k=1,\dots,8\) as features. The true solution (dashed) is a broad hump plus smaller wiggles at \(k=3, 5, 8\). Gradient descent on the physics loss starts from \(u=0\) with the largest stable step. The slider sets the number of steps on a log scale. The three options choose a preconditioner, a fixed diagonal rescaling of each mode's step:

Plain: no rescaling, \(\kappa=4096\). Divide by \((k\pi)^2\): undoes one of the two operator factors, \(\kappa=64\). Divide by \((k\pi)^4\): undoes both, which is exactly Newton's method here, \(\kappa=1\).

Try: on Plain, drag from 1 step up. Notice: the fine wiggles appear almost immediately, but the big hump needs thousands of steps; the bars show mode 1 stuck near full error. Switch to \((k\pi)^2\) and the same accuracy arrives about 64 times sooner. The fix is entirely about the spectrum of \(H\), not the network.

IV · Cool Things

ConnectionsMethods the review surveys

Physics-informed Gaussian processes are exact linear algebrablock PSD kernel

The review covers kernel methods alongside networks. If \(u\) is a Gaussian process (a random function whose values at any finite set of points are jointly Gaussian) with covariance \(k(x,x')\), and \(\mathcal L\) is a linear differential operator, then \(\mathcal Lu\) is Gaussian too, and

$$\operatorname{Cov}\begin{pmatrix}u\\ \mathcal Lu\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}k & \mathcal L_{x'}k\\ \mathcal L_xk & \mathcal L_x\mathcal L_{x'}k\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0 .$$

Observing \(f=\mathcal Lu\) at some points and \(u\) at others, the prediction is one solve against this block Gram matrix. It is the same \(\mathcal N_x\mathcal N_{x'}\) structure as the NTK's PDE block, without any training.

Operator learning is a rank-p expansionDeepONet

Instead of solving one PDE, learn the whole solution map \(G\): input function \(v\) (a source, an initial condition) to solution \(G(v)\). DeepONet, from co-author Lu Lu, writes

$$G(v)(y)\approx\sum_{k=1}^{p}b_k(v)\,t_k(y),$$

where a "branch" network reads \(v\) at fixed sensor points and outputs coefficients \(b_k\), and a "trunk" network outputs basis functions \(t_k\) of the location \(y\). That is a learned rank-\(p\) separable expansion. For a linear compact \(G\), the best such expansion is the truncated singular value decomposition, so DeepONet is a nonlinear cousin of truncated SVD and of proper orthogonal decomposition.

Hard constraints delete a whole blockinductive bias

Write \(u_\theta(x)=x(1-x)\,\mathrm{NN}_\theta(x)\), with \(\mathrm{NN}_\theta\) any network. Now \(u(0)=u(1)=0\) for every \(\theta\): the boundary residual is identically zero, \(K_{bb}\) disappears, and so does the imbalance problem. Exact constraints restrict the search to an affine family; soft penalties only push toward it.

Inverse problems cost one extra parametersame code

If the diffusivity \(\nu\) in \(u_t=\nu u_{xx}\) is unknown, append it to \(\theta\) and train on data plus residual as before. Classical inverse solvers need adjoint equations and separate code; here the forward and inverse problems are one loss. That uniformity is the review's strongest selling point.

V · If You Remember One Thing

The one thing to remember

A PINN turns "solve this PDE" into least squares on PDE residuals at scattered points. The price is that the loss inherits the spectrum of \(\mathcal N^*\mathcal N\), with condition number growing like \(k^4\) for a second-order operator, so training is a preconditioning problem as much as a learning one.
