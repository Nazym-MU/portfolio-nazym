Tensor Decomposition

Tensor Decomposition for Signal Processing and Machine Learning

Nicholas D. Sidiropoulos, Lieven De Lathauwer, Xiao Fu et al. · 2016 · arXiv:1607.01668

A long tutorial on data with three or more indices (say user × movie × time). It shows how to split such data into a sum of simple “rank-one” pieces, and why that split, unlike any low-rank factorization of an ordinary matrix, is usually unique.

I · In 60 Seconds

Problem\(AB^\top = (AM)(BM^{-\top})^\top\)

Lots of data has three indices: ratings indexed by user, movie and time; radio samples by receiver antenna, code chip and transmitted symbol; word triples by word, word, word. The usual move is to flatten it into a matrix and fit a low-rank model \(X\approx AB^\top\), hoping the columns of \(A\) and \(B\) mean something: one speaker, one user type, one topic.

They can't, in general. For any invertible \(M\), the pair \(AM\) and \(BM^{-\top}\) fits the data equally well. So a matrix factorization pins down a subspace, never the individual factors. You can't separate two mixed signals that way without extra assumptions such as orthogonality, which real sources rarely satisfy.

Ideasum of rank-one tensors

Keep the third index. Write the three-way array as a sum of \(F\) “rank-one” arrays, each an outer product of three vectors: entry \((i,j,k)\) of piece \(f\) is \(a_f(i)\,b_f(j)\,c_f(k)\). This is the canonical polyadic decomposition (CPD), also called PARAFAC.

Seen slice by slice, every matrix \(X_k\) (third index fixed at \(k\)) is \(A\,D_k\,B^\top\). The same \(A\) and \(B\) appear in every slice; only a diagonal weighting \(D_k\) changes. That shared structure leaves no room for an arbitrary \(M\).

Result\(k_A+k_B+k_C\ge 2F+2\)

Under mild conditions, the factors are unique up to the trivial freedom of reordering the pieces and rescaling inside each one. For example, a generic \(I\times J\times K\) array with \(I,J\ge F\) and only \(K=2\) slices already has a unique decomposition. Kruskal's condition (Section II) allows even more pieces than any side length: generic \(10\times10\times10\) arrays are unique up to \(F=14\).

The price is that the matrix intuitions fail elsewhere. Computing tensor rank is NP-hard. A best low-rank approximation may not exist. A random \(2\times2\times2\) real array has rank 2 with probability \(\pi/4\approx0.785\) and rank 3 otherwise. The paper covers the theory, algorithms (alternating least squares, Gauss–Newton, stochastic gradient, ADMM), error bounds, and applications from blind radio decoding to topic models.

II · The Linear Algebra

MachinerySlices share factors

The objects

\(\mathcal X\in\mathbb R^{I\times J\times K}\)the data

A three-way array with entries \(x_{ijk}\). Its frontal slices \(X_k := \mathcal X(:,:,k)\) are \(K\) ordinary \(I\times J\) matrices.

\(A, B, C\)\(I\times F,\ J\times F,\ K\times F\)

The factor matrices. Column \(f\) of each (\(a_f, b_f, c_f\)) builds piece \(f\). They are dense and unconstrained by default; the paper also covers non-negative, sparse, orthogonal, Vandermonde (columns \(1, z, z^2,\dots\)) and other structured versions.

\(a\circ b\circ c\)rank one

The outer product, with entries \(a(i)b(j)c(k)\). For two vectors it is just \(ab^\top\). The rank of \(\mathcal X\) is the least \(F\) such that \(\mathcal X\) is a sum of \(F\) of these.

$$\mathcal X=\sum_{f=1}^{F} a_f\circ b_f\circ c_f \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad X_k = A\,D_k(C)\,B^\top,\qquad D_k(C):=\operatorname{Diag}\big(C(k,:)\big).$$

Stacking the vectorised slices as columns gives the mode-3 unfolding, an ordinary \(IJ\times K\) matrix:

$$X_{(3)} := \big[\operatorname{vec}X_1,\dots,\operatorname{vec}X_K\big] = (B\odot A)\,C^\top,\qquad B\odot A := [\,b_1\otimes a_1,\ \dots,\ b_F\otimes a_F\,].$$

Here \(\otimes\) is the Kronecker product and \(\odot\) the Khatri–Rao product, which pairs only matching columns. It is the \(F\)-column subset of \(B\otimes A\), coming from \(\operatorname{vec}(A\,\mathrm{Diag}(d)\,B^\top)=(B\odot A)\,d\). So, flattened, a rank-\(F\) tensor is a rank-\(F\) matrix whose left factor has a rigid structure: every column is itself a Kronecker product. Hiding in that structure is everything matrices lack.

The spectral fact that does the work

Take the simplest case: two slices, \(I=J=F\), with \(A\), \(B\) and \(D_1\) invertible. Then

$$X_2X_1^{-1} = A D_2 B^\top\,B^{-\top} D_1^{-1}A^{-1} = A\,\big(D_2D_1^{-1}\big)\,A^{-1}.$$

That is an eigendecomposition. The columns of \(A\) are the eigenvectors of the (non-symmetric) matrix \(X_2X_1^{-1}\), and the eigenvalues are the ratios \(c_{2f}/c_{1f}\). Eigenvectors are determined exactly up to scale and order, provided the eigenvalues are distinct. That leftover freedom is precisely the permutation and scaling ambiguity, and nothing else. Once \(A\) is known, \(B^\top = (A D_1)^{-1}X_1\) follows.

If two ratios coincide, i.e. two columns of \(C\) are proportional, the eigenspace becomes 2-dimensional and any basis of it works: uniqueness breaks for exactly those pieces. The general case (\(I,J\ge F\), rectangular) runs the same argument on the generalized eigenproblem \(X_2 e = \lambda X_1 e\) with pseudo-inverses. With \(K>2\) slices you apply it to two random linear combinations of the slices.

Tiny example: 2 × 2 × 2, F = 2

Take \(A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\), \(B=I\), and \(C=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\) (row \(k\) of \(C\) gives the weights for slice \(k\)). Then \(D_1=I\), \(D_2=\mathrm{Diag}(1,2)\), and the data is

$$X_1=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad X_2=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&2\end{pmatrix}.$$

On its own, \(X_1\) has infinitely many rank-2 factorizations (\(X_1 = I\cdot X_1 = A\cdot I = (AM)M^{-1}\), …). But

$$X_2X_1^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix},$$

with eigenvalue \(1\) on \((1,0)^\top\) and \(2\) on \((1,1)^\top\): exactly the columns of \(A\), with ratios \(c_{2f}/c_{1f}=1,2\). Then \(B^\top=A^{-1}X_1=I\). The second slice removed every bit of freedom.

Over \(\mathbb R\) there is a twist. \(X_2X_1^{-1}\) is real but not symmetric, so its eigenvalues may be a complex-conjugate pair. Then no real rank-2 decomposition exists, and the real rank is at least 3 (in fact exactly 3 for \(2\times2\times2\)). For Gaussian random slices this happens with probability \(1-\pi/4\). Over \(\mathbb C\) the rank is 2 almost surely. Tensor rank depends on the field, and real tensors can have several “typical” ranks, each with positive probability. You can sample this in Section III.

Kruskal rank: the general uniqueness condition

The Kruskal rank \(k_A\) is the largest \(k\) such that every set of \(k\) columns of \(A\) is linearly independent. It always satisfies \(k_A\le\operatorname{rank}A\). (In compressed sensing the same quantity appears as spark − 1.)

$$k_A+k_B+k_C\;\ge\;2F+2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \operatorname{rank}\mathcal X=F\ \text{and the CPD is essentially unique.}$$

Check it against the eigen argument: if \(A\) and \(B\) have full column rank, then \(k_A=k_B=F\) and the condition reduces to \(k_C\ge2\), i.e. no two columns of \(C\) are proportional, i.e. distinct eigenvalues. The bound is sharp. Generic matrices have \(k=\min(\text{rows},F)\), so for \(10\times10\times10\) tensors \(30\ge2F+2\) gives uniqueness up to \(F=14\) pieces, more than any side length. A matrix can never have rank above its smallest dimension. The paper includes a one-page proof of a weaker version (\(r_C=F\), \(k_A+k_B\ge F+2\)) built on Sylvester's rank inequality and a “permutation lemma”: a matrix that maps every weight-one vector to a weight-one vector is a scaled permutation.

The other decomposition: Tucker and the multilinear SVD

CPD lets only matching columns interact. The Tucker model lets every column of \(U\) (size \(I\times r_1\)) interact with every column of \(V\) (\(J\times r_2\)) and of \(W\) (\(K\times r_3\)), with strengths stored in a small core array \(\mathcal G\) of size \(r_1\times r_2\times r_3\):

$$x_{ijk}=\sum_{\ell,m,n} g_{\ell mn}\,u_{i\ell}\,v_{jm}\,w_{kn}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \operatorname{vec}\mathcal X=(U\otimes V\otimes W)\,\operatorname{vec}\mathcal G .$$

The multilinear SVD (MLSVD, also called HOSVD) takes \(U\) to be the top left singular vectors of the mode-1 unfolding (the \(I\times JK\) matrix whose columns are the fibers \(\mathcal X(:,j,k)\)), and likewise for \(V, W\). The \(r_n\) are the ranks of those unfoldings (the multilinear rank). Since \(U\otimes V\otimes W\) has orthonormal columns by the mixed-product rule \((U\otimes V)^\top(U\otimes V)=U^\top U\otimes V^\top V=I\), it is an isometry:

$$\|\mathcal X\|_F=\|\mathcal G\|_F,\qquad \|\mathcal X-\hat{\mathcal X}\|_F^2=\sum_{\text{dropped}} g_{\ell mn}^2 .$$

A diagonal core is impossible in general: a diagonal core would make \(\mathcal X\) a sum of \(\min(I,J,K)\) rank-one terms, and tensor rank can be far larger. What survives is all-orthogonality. Slabs \(\mathcal G(\ell,:,:)\) are mutually orthogonal, and \(\|\mathcal G(\ell,:,:)\|_F\) is the \(\ell\)-th singular value of the mode-1 unfolding (same for other modes). There is no Eckart–Young theorem, though. Truncating the MLSVD is not optimal, only within a factor \(N\) (here 3) of the optimal squared error. Finding the best low-multilinear-rank fit, even rank \((1,1,1)\), is NP-hard. Tucker is not unique either (\(U\mapsto UM\), with \(M^{-1}\) absorbed into the core). It serves compression and subspaces; CPD serves separation.

Computing a CPD: least squares with a Hadamard shortcut

Fitting is trilinear, hence non-convex, but linear in each factor alone. Alternating least squares cycles through \(C^\top\leftarrow (B\odot A)^\dagger X_{(3)}\) and its two cousins. The normal matrix collapses to a tiny one:

$$(B\odot A)^\top(B\odot A) = (B^\top B)\ast(A^\top A)\qquad(\ast = \text{entrywise product, } F\times F).$$

Each update therefore inverts one \(F\times F\) matrix. The real cost is the product \((B\odot A)^\top X_{(3)}\), which can be computed without forming the \(IJ\times F\) Khatri–Rao matrix and in time linear in the number of nonzeros of sparse data.

III · Try It: Unmix Two Slices

InteractiveEigenvectors are the factors

A hidden \(2\times2\times2\) tensor is built from two true directions \(a_1, a_2\) (solid lines). Slice 1 weights both pieces by 1. Slice 2 weights piece 1 by 1 and piece 2 by \(s\). A small fixed noise pattern of size \(\varepsilon\) is added to both slices. The page computes the eigenvectors of \(X_2X_1^{-1}\) and draws them dashed.

Try: start at \(s=2.5\), then drag \(s\) slowly to \(1\). Notice: far from 1 the dashed lines sit on the solid ones. As the two eigenvalues \(1\) and \(s\) approach each other, the recovered directions swing away, because the noise is amplified roughly like \(1/|s-1|\). Near \(s=1\) the eigenvalues turn complex: this noisy tensor has no real rank-2 decomposition, its real rank is 3. Set \(\varepsilon=0\) and \(s=1\) exactly, and every direction is an eigenvector, which is the matrix-style non-uniqueness.

How often is a random tensor rank 2?

Each click draws random \(2\times2\times2\) tensors with independent standard Gaussian entries. A tensor counts as real rank 2 when the pencil \(\det(X_2-\lambda X_1)=0\) has two real roots; otherwise it is rank 3. Notice: the rank-2 share settles near \(\pi/4\approx0.785\), not 1. For random matrices one rank shows up with probability 1. For real tensors, two ranks each show up a positive fraction of the time.

IV · Cool Things

FindingsFrom the paper, plus one link

The best rank-2 fit doesn't existborder rank

For unit vectors \(u,v\) that aren't parallel, \(\mathcal X=u\circ u\circ v+u\circ v\circ u+v\circ u\circ u\) has rank 3. Yet

$$\mathcal X_n=n\big(u+\tfrac1n v\big)^{\circ3}-n\,u^{\circ3}=\mathcal X+O(1/n),$$

a rank-2 sequence that gets arbitrarily close. The set of rank-\(\le2\) tensors isn't closed, so “minimise the error over rank 2” has an infimum of 0 and no minimiser. Fitting algorithms hit this as two components that grow without bound while cancelling each other. Remedies: constraints (non-negativity, orthogonality) or norm penalties. Quantum aside (not in the paper): with \(u=|0\rangle,\ v=|1\rangle\), \(\mathcal X\) is the unnormalised three-qubit W state \(|001\rangle+|010\rangle+|100\rangle\). That is the standard example of a state that has tensor rank 3 but is a limit of rank-2 (GHZ-type) states.

Tensor rank = number of multiplications4 → 3, 8 → 7

A bilinear map is a 3-way tensor, and a rank-\(F\) decomposition with entries in \(\{0,\pm1\}\) computes it with \(F\) multiplications. Complex multiplication \((x_r+ix_i)(y_r+iy_i)\) has slices \(\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\), and its rank is 3. That gives \(p_1=x_ry_r,\ p_2=x_iy_i,\ p_3=(x_r+x_i)(y_r+y_i)\), then real part \(p_1-p_2\) and imaginary part \(p_3-p_1-p_2\). The \(4\times4\times4\) tensor for multiplying \(2\times2\) matrices has rank 7, not 8: that is Strassen's algorithm.

Third moments find what covariances can'tmixtures, topics

Take a mixture of \(F\) spherical Gaussians with means \(\mu_f\) (columns of \(M\)) and weights \(\pi_f\). The second moment is \(M\,\mathrm{Diag}(\pi)M^\top+\bar\sigma^2 I\), which is symmetric, PSD and low-rank, but determines \(M\) only up to a rotation. After subtracting a known correction, the third-moment tensor is \(\sum_f\pi_f\,\mu_f\circ\mu_f\circ\mu_f\): a symmetric CPD, unique under the conditions above. Topic models work the same way: the probabilities of word triples drawn from one document form the CPD \(\sum_f\pi_f\,p_f\circ p_f\circ p_f\), whose factors \(p_f\) are the per-topic word distributions.

Decoding radio without the codesblind CDMA

In code-division radio, user \(f\) spreads its bit stream \(b_f\) with a secret code \(s_f\). One receiver sees \(Y=SB^\top\), a matrix, so the users can't be separated without knowing \(S\). Two receivers at different spots see \(Y_i=S\,D_i(H)\,B^\top\), where \(H(i,f)\) is the path loss from user \(f\) to receiver \(i\). That is a CPD, so codes, bits and path losses all come out uniquely, even with more users than the code length.

V · If You Remember One Thing

The one thing to remember

A matrix factorization \(AB^\top\) is never unique, but once a third index gives you slices \(X_k=A\,D_k\,B^\top\) that share the same factors, \(X_2X_1^{-1}=A(D_2D_1^{-1})A^{-1}\) is an eigendecomposition, and its eigenvectors are the factors.

Tensor Decomposition for Signal Processing and Machine Learning, by Nicholas D. Sidiropoulos, Lieven De Lathauwer, Xiao Fu, Kejun Huang, Evangelos E. Papalexakis and Christos Faloutsos, 2016. arxiv.org/abs/1607.01668
