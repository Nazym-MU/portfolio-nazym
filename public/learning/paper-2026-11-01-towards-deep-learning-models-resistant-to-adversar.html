Paper of the day · ICLR 2018

The Worst-Case Box

Towards Deep Learning Models Resistant to Adversarial Attacks

Aleksander Mądry, Aleksandar Makelov, Ludwig Schmidt et al. · 2017 · arXiv:1706.06083

Image classifiers can be fooled by changes to the pixels too small for a person to see. This paper states what “robust” should mean as a min–max problem: train the network to have low loss at the worst image inside a small box around each training image. It then shows the inner “find the worst image” problem can be solved well enough by repeated signed gradient steps, and that training on those worst images gives the first networks that hold up against strong attacks.

I · In 60 Seconds

Problem99.2% → 6.4%

A network trained on MNIST (28×28 grey images of handwritten digits, pixels scaled to [0, 1]) gets 99.2% of test digits right. Move every pixel by at most 0.3, in the direction the network's gradient suggests, and accuracy falls to 6.4%. Such inputs are called adversarial examples.

Many defences had been proposed, but each was tested against particular known attacks, and new attacks kept breaking them. Nobody could say what a defence actually guaranteed.

Ideamin over weights, max over the box

Fix the attacker's power first: it may change each pixel by at most \(\varepsilon\), i.e. pick any image in a small box around the real one. Then train to minimise the loss at the worst image in each box. The inner maximisation is the attack; the outer minimisation is training.

For the inner problem they use projected gradient descent (PGD, run uphill): take a small step in the direction of the sign of the loss gradient, clip back into the box, repeat. Experiments show its answers from random starts all reach similar loss, so PGD is treated as the strongest attack that uses only gradients. Training then just feeds PGD's images to ordinary gradient descent.

Result89.3% under attack

On MNIST with \(\varepsilon = 0.3\), the robust network is 98.8% accurate on clean digits and 89.3% against the strongest attack tried (PGD, 100 steps, 20 random restarts). On CIFAR-10 (32×32 colour photos of 10 object classes; \(\varepsilon = 8\) on the 0–255 pixel scale) it reaches 87.3% clean and 45.8% under attack. A normally trained network of the same size gets 3.5%.

Robustness needs capacity: small networks trained this way collapse to predicting one class, and wider ones do steadily better.

II · The Linear Algebra

MachineryA cube, its dual norm, and a max-function

The objects

\(x\in\mathbb R^{d},\ y\)one image, its label

An image flattened to a vector: \(d = 784\) for MNIST, \(d = 32\cdot32\cdot3 = 3072\) for CIFAR-10. The label \(y\) is one of 10 classes. Pairs \((x,y)\) are drawn from the data distribution \(\mathcal D\).

\(\theta\in\mathbb R^{p}\), \(L(\theta,x,y)\)weights, loss

\(\theta\) is every weight of the network. \(L\) is the cross-entropy loss: minus the log of the probability the network gives the correct label. It is non-convex in \(\theta\), non-concave in \(x\), and only piecewise smooth (ReLU and max-pooling have kinks).

\(\mathcal S=\{\delta:\|\delta\|_\infty\le\varepsilon\}\)the cube \([-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]^d\)

The attacker's allowed changes \(\delta\): every pixel moves by at most \(\varepsilon\). It is a product of \(d\) intervals, a hypercube with \(2^d\) corners \(\varepsilon s\), \(s\in\{\pm1\}^d\). Being a product is what makes everything below cheap.

The whole paper is about one saddle-point problem (their eq. 2.1):

$$\min_{\theta}\ \rho(\theta),\qquad \rho(\theta)=\mathbb E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal D}\Big[\max_{\delta\in\mathcal S} L(\theta,\,x+\delta,\,y)\Big].$$

\(\rho\) is the robust risk: the expected loss when an adversary moves each input to the worst point in its box. If \(\rho(\theta)\) is small, no allowed perturbation of a typical input causes high loss. That is a guarantee against every attack in the class, not just the ones you tried.

The fact doing the work: the cube's dual norm is \(\ell_1\)

Linearise the loss around \(x\): \(L(x+\delta)\approx L(x)+g^{\top}\delta\), with \(g=\nabla_xL(\theta,x,y)\) the gradient with respect to the input. Maximising a linear function over the cube is solved coordinate by coordinate:

$$\max_{\|\delta\|_\infty\le\varepsilon} g^{\top}\delta=\varepsilon\|g\|_1,\qquad \text{attained at }\ \delta^\star=\varepsilon\,\operatorname{sgn}(g).$$

(Proof: \(g^\top\delta\le\sum_i|g_i||\delta_i|\le\varepsilon\sum_i|g_i|\), with equality at the sign vector.) The maximiser is a corner of the cube. Over the Euclidean ball the answer would be \(\varepsilon\|g\|_2\) at \(\varepsilon g/\|g\|_2\). Since \(\|g\|_1\) can be as large as \(\sqrt d\,\|g\|_2\), the box is a much stronger attacker in high dimension.

That one line is the earlier one-step attack, the fast gradient sign method (FGSM): \(x+\varepsilon\operatorname{sgn}(\nabla_xL)\). It is the exact solution of the linearised inner problem, and no better than the linearisation.

Tiny example

\(d=2\), \(g=(3,-1)\), \(\varepsilon=0.1\). Box: \(\delta^\star=(0.1,-0.1)\), gain \(g^\top\delta^\star=0.4=\varepsilon\|g\|_1\). Ball: \(\delta=0.1\,(3,-1)/\sqrt{10}\), gain \(0.1\sqrt{10}\approx0.316\). The cube's corner sticks out further along \(g\) than the ball does.

Robust margin of a linear classifier: the same duality

Take the simplest classifier, \(\operatorname{sign}(w^\top x-b)\). The adversary can lower the score by \(\max_{\|\delta\|_\infty\le\varepsilon}w^\top\delta=\varepsilon\|w\|_1\). So \(x\) is safe iff \(|w^\top x-b|>\varepsilon\|w\|_1\). Equivalently, its \(\ell_\infty\)-distance to the hyperplane \(\{w^\top z=b\}\) is \(|w^\top x-b|/\|w\|_1\). In general the distance in a norm is the score divided by the dual norm of \(w\).

Tiny example

\(w=(1,1),\ b=0,\ x=(0.2,0.1)\), score \(0.3\), \(\varepsilon=0.2\). The worst \(\delta=-0.2\,(1,1)\) gives score \(-0.1\): flipped. Here \(\ell_\infty\)-distance \(0.3/\|w\|_1=0.15<0.2\), while the Euclidean distance \(0.3/\sqrt2\approx0.21>0.2\), so a ball of radius 0.2 would have been safe.

This is the paper's Figure 3 picture. A line can separate the points and still cut through their boxes, so a robust classifier needs a more complicated boundary. That is why capacity matters.

The attack: PGD is sign-ascent plus clipping

The inner problem is not linear, so they iterate the one-step answer. With step size \(\alpha\) and \(\Pi_{x+\mathcal S}\) the Euclidean projection onto the box around \(x\):

$$x^{t+1}=\Pi_{x+\mathcal S}\big(x^{t}+\alpha\,\operatorname{sgn}(\nabla_xL(\theta,x^{t},y))\big),\qquad \Pi_{x+\mathcal S}(z)_i=\min\!\big(\max(z_i,\,x_i-\varepsilon),\,x_i+\varepsilon\big).$$

Two structural facts make each step cost one gradient plus \(O(d)\):

The sign step is steepest ascent in the \(\ell_\infty\) geometry: \(\operatorname{sgn}(g)\) maximises \(g^\top v\) over \(\|v\|_\infty\le1\), by the duality above.

The projection onto a product of intervals splits into independent 1-D projections, i.e. clipping each pixel. (Onto an \(\ell_2\) ball you would rescale; onto an \(\ell_1\) ball you would need a sort.)

The paper starts from a uniformly random point in the box. MNIST: 40 steps of \(\alpha=0.01\) with \(\varepsilon=0.3\). CIFAR-10: 7 steps of size 2 with \(\varepsilon=8\).

Tiny example: one PGD step

\(x=(0.50,\,0.95)\), \(\varepsilon=0.1\): box \([0.40,0.60]\times[0.85,1.05]\). Current \(x^t=(0.58,\,0.97)\), gradient \((2.0,\,-0.3)\), \(\alpha=0.05\). Sign step: \((0.63,\,0.92)\). Clip: \((0.60,\,0.92)\). The first coordinate hits the wall; the second moves freely.

Why training on attacked inputs is the right gradient: Danskin

Fix one example and write \(\phi(\theta)=\max_{\delta\in\mathcal S}L(\theta,x+\delta,y)\), a pointwise max of a compact family of functions. Training needs \(\nabla\phi\). Danskin's theorem (their Appendix A): if \(\nabla_\theta L\) is continuous, then \(\phi\) is directionally differentiable with

$$\phi'(\theta;h)=\sup_{\delta\in\delta^\ast(\theta)}h^{\top}\nabla_\theta L(\theta,x+\delta,y),\qquad \delta^\ast(\theta)=\arg\max_{\delta\in\mathcal S}L(\theta,x+\delta,y).$$

If the maximiser \(\delta^\ast\) is unique, \(\nabla\phi(\theta)=\nabla_\theta L(\theta,x+\delta^\ast,y)\). So you may treat the attacked image as fixed data and backpropagate as usual. The max moving with \(\theta\) contributes nothing to first order (the envelope theorem).

Tiny example: why uniqueness matters

\(L(\theta,\delta)=\theta\delta\), \(\delta\in[-1,1]\), so \(\phi(\theta)=|\theta|\). At \(\theta=2\), \(\delta^\ast=1\) and Danskin gives \(\phi'(2)=\delta^\ast=1\). Correct. At \(\theta=0\) every \(\delta\) is a maximiser, and \(\phi'(0;h)=\sup_{\delta}h\delta=|h|>0\) in both directions. That is a kink, with no descent direction.

The paper is upfront that the hypotheses fail: ReLUs make \(L\) non-smooth, and PGD returns a local, not global, maximiser. Their patch is to restrict to a sub-box \(\mathcal S'\) where the found maximiser is global. Then the step at least decreases the loss at that adversarial point. The real evidence is empirical: the adversarial training loss falls steadily (their Figure 5).

The surprising landscape of the inner problem

The inner max is highly non-concave, so you'd expect PGD to get stuck at bad local maxima. They ran PGD from \(10^5\) random starts per image. The maxima it found were genuinely different points: pairwise angles near 90°, distances like random pairs in the box. But their loss values were tightly concentrated, with no outliers. Along the segment between two maxima, the loss is convex and stays well above a random point's.

The 90° is ordinary high-dimensional geometry. For independent \(u,v\) uniform in \([-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]^d\), \(\cos\angle(u,v)\) has mean 0 and standard deviation about \(1/\sqrt d\), i.e. \(\pm2^\circ\) for \(d=784\). The concentration of the values is the real empirical finding. It is why PGD is offered as a “universal first-order adversary”: one that uses only gradients probably can't do much better.

2 · One jump (FGSM) vs PGD with restarts

A made-up loss over a 2-D perturbation \(\delta\), drawn as ink contour lines. The clean input \(x\) is at the centre and the bold square is the box of half-width \(\varepsilon\). The loss rises gently to the upper right, and there is a steep hill down-left that the gradient at \(x\) cannot see. The dashed arrow and hollow square are FGSM: one jump to the corner \(\varepsilon\operatorname{sgn}(\nabla L(x))\). The thin paths are 16 PGD runs from random starts (40 sign steps of size \(2.5\varepsilon/40\), clipped to the box, the paper's step rule). The ringed dot is the best endpoint.

Try: start at \(\varepsilon=0.25\), then raise it to 0.5 and beyond; press “new starts” a few times. Notice: for small \(\varepsilon\) the loss is nearly linear inside the box, so FGSM and PGD agree. Past about 0.35 the hill enters the box: FGSM still jumps to the upper-right corner, but PGD runs that start nearby climb the hill to much higher loss. This is the paper's point that FGSM is a fine attack only when \(\varepsilon\) is small. IV · Cool Things

FindingsFrom the paper's tables and appendix

Training on FGSM makes you better under attack than clean, and useless against PGD90.9% / 0.0%

A CIFAR-10 network trained on FGSM images scores 87.4% on clean images, 90.9% on FGSM images, and 0.0% against 7-step PGD. FGSM's perturbations are a single, predictable corner, so the network learns to recognise them (“label leaking”) rather than become robust. A defence evaluated only against its own attack proves nothing.

The robust MNIST net learned a sparse thresholding layer3 of 32 filters

In the first convolution layer, only 3 of 32 filters are used, each with a single nonzero weight. A convolution with a one-entry kernel is just a scalar multiple of the (shifted) image, so the layer becomes \(\mathrm{ReLU}(\alpha x_i-\beta)\) per pixel: three thresholded copies of the image. A pixel with \(x_i+\varepsilon\le\beta/\alpha\) outputs 0 whatever the attacker does. Nobody coded this in. The authors tried hand-building the same tricks without adversarial training, and PGD fooled the result on every test example.

Many maxima, nearly orthogonal, same height\(10^5\) restarts

A non-concave problem whose local maxima sit at nearly 90° to each other and yet all reach nearly the same loss. It mirrors the folklore about training itself, where many different minima have similar loss. This is what licenses treating PGD as “the” first-order attack.

Gradient masking: the thresholds fool the attacker too\(\ell_2\), \(\varepsilon=4.5\)

Under \(\ell_2\)-bounded attacks, PGD seemed unable to fool the MNIST model even at radius 4.5, where the digits visibly change identity. The paper itself flags this as an overestimate. A ReLU stuck at 0 has zero gradient, so the thresholds hide the direction to attack. An attack that uses no gradients (the decision-boundary attack) shows the model is far more brittle in \(\ell_2\). Robustness claimed under one norm doesn't transfer to another.

V · If You Remember One Thing

The one thing to remember

Robust training is \(\min_\theta\mathbb E\max_{\|\delta\|_\infty\le\varepsilon}L\). Because the box's dual norm is \(\ell_1\), the best linear attack is the corner \(\varepsilon\operatorname{sgn}(\nabla_xL)\); PGD repeats that step and clips. By Danskin's theorem, ordinary training on PGD's outputs is gradient descent on the worst-case loss.
