LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models

Edward J. Hu, Yelong Shen, Phillip Wallis et al. · 2021 · ICLR 2022 · arXiv:2106.09685

To fine-tune a huge pretrained model, freeze every weight matrix \(W_0\) and learn only a rank-\(r\) correction \(\Delta W = BA\). The paper shows that \(r = 1\)–\(4\) is often enough.

In 60 seconds

  • Problem. Full fine-tuning of GPT-3 learns a new \(\Delta\Phi\) with all 175B entries for every task. That means a 350 GB checkpoint per task and 1.2 TB of GPU memory to train. Adapter layers avoid this, but they add 20–30% latency at batch size 1.
  • Idea. Write the update to each attention matrix as a product \(BA\) with \(B\in\mathbb R^{d\times r}\), \(A\in\mathbb R^{r\times k}\), \(r\ll d\), and train only \(A, B\). After training, fold \(BA\) into \(W_0\).
  • Result. On GPT-3 175B, 4.7M trainable parameters (≈0.003%) match or beat full fine-tuning: MNLI 91.7 vs 89.5, WikiSQL 73.4 vs 73.8, SAMSum ROUGE-1 53.8 vs 52.0. The checkpoint drops to 35 MB, training memory to 350 GB, and inference latency is unchanged.

The linear algebra

The objects

\(W_0\in\mathbb R^{d\times k}\)
Pretrained, dense, generically full-rank, frozen. In GPT-3 these are \(W_q, W_v\) with \(d=k=d_{\text{model}}=12{,}288\), in each of 96 layers.
\(B\in\mathbb R^{d\times r}\)
Trainable, initialised to \(0\).
\(A\in\mathbb R^{r\times k}\)
Trainable, initialised i.i.d. \(\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\).
\(\Delta W = BA\)
\(\operatorname{rank}\le r\), with \(r\in\{1,2,4,8\}\). It is zero at initialisation, so training starts exactly at the pretrained model.
$$h \;=\; W_0x + \frac{\alpha}{r}\,BAx, \qquad \#\text{params} = r(d+k)\ \text{ instead of } dk.$$

For one GPT-3 matrix with \(r=1\), that is 24,576 numbers instead of 151 million. The \(\alpha/r\) factor keeps the update's scale stable when you change \(r\), so the learning rate doesn't need re-tuning.

At deployment you store \(W = W_0 + BA\). It has the same shape as \(W_0\), so a forward pass costs exactly what the base model's does. To switch tasks, subtract \(BA\) and add \(B'A'\).

The fact doing the work: the update's spectrum decays fast

Take the SVD of the update and apply Eckart–Young:

$$\Delta W=\sum_{i}\sigma_i\,u_iv_i^{\top},\qquad \min_{\operatorname{rank}M\le r}\|\Delta W-M\|_F^2=\sum_{i>r}\sigma_i^2 .$$

LoRA's bet is that the tail \(\sum_{i>r}\sigma_i^2\) is negligible for the task update, even though \(W_0\) itself is full-rank. LoRA never computes an SVD during training; it learns the factors directly. The SVD appears only in Section 7, where they check the bet. At \(r=8\) and \(r=64\), the top singular direction of the learned \(A\) is shared (similarity \(>0.5\)). The remaining directions don't agree across ranks or random seeds, which suggests they are mostly noise.

What you already have

  • you know thisRank. \(\operatorname{rank}(BA)\le r\) is the entire constraint. Nothing else, such as sparsity or orthogonality, is imposed on \(A\) or \(B\).
  • you know thisSVD / low-rank approximation. Used only to analyse the learned \(A\), via its top right singular vectors in \(\mathbb R^{12288}\). Eckart–Young bounds what any rank-\(r\) \(BA\) could achieve.
  • you know thisProjections and Frobenius norm. To ask how \(\Delta W\) relates to \(W\), they project \(W\) onto \(\Delta W\)'s top-\(r\) singular subspaces: \(\|U_r^{\top} W V_r\|_F\). They compare that with \(\|\Delta W\|_F\) (see “Cool things”).
  • you know thisAttention. Only \(W_q, W_v\) get LoRA. The MLP, \(W_k\) and \(W_o\) stay frozen.
  • you know thisGradient descent. Only \(A\) and \(B\) receive gradients, which is where the memory savings come from.
new to you

Gradients through a factorisation (and why \(B=0\) at init)

If the loss depends on \(W = W_0 + BA\) and \(G = \partial L/\partial W\in\mathbb R^{d\times k}\), the chain rule gives

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial B}=G A^{\top},\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial A}=B^{\top}G,\qquad \delta(BA)\approx-\eta\big(G\,A^{\top}\!A+BB^{\top}G\big).$$

\(A^{\top}A\) (size \(k\times k\)) and \(BB^{\top}\) (size \(d\times d\)) are PSD with rank \(\le r\). So each LoRA step is the full fine-tuning step \(-\eta G\), multiplied on the right and left by two low-rank PSD matrices. The step only sees the part of \(G\) that lies on \(\operatorname{row}(A)\) and \(\operatorname{col}(B)\).

Tiny example

\(d=k=2,\ r=1\): \(A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\end{pmatrix},\ B=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix},\ G=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\).

\(\partial L/\partial A = B^{\top}G = (0\ \ 0)\), so \(A\) does not move on step 1. \(\partial L/\partial B = GA^{\top} = \binom{1}{-2}\). With \(\eta = 0.1\) we get \(B=\binom{-0.1}{0.2}\), so \(\Delta W = BA=\begin{pmatrix}-0.1&-0.2\\0.2&0.4\end{pmatrix} = -\eta\,G A^{\top}A\). Full fine-tuning would have stepped \(-\eta G=\begin{pmatrix}-0.1&0\\0&0.1\end{pmatrix}\), which is rank 2. LoRA keeps only \(G\)'s action on \(\operatorname{span}\{(1,2)\}\), scaled by \(\|A\|^2=5\).

Intuition: because \(B=0\), LoRA starts as gradient descent inside a random \(r\)-dimensional slice of input space, spanned by the Gaussian rows of \(A\). This is the “random subspace / intrinsic dimension” picture that inspired the paper. After the first step \(A\) moves too, so the slice itself is learned.

new to you

Principal angles between subspaces (the paper's \(\phi\))

Let \(U\in\mathbb R^{n\times i}\) and \(V\in\mathbb R^{n\times j}\) have orthonormal columns. The singular values of \(U^{\top}V\) are \(\cos\theta_1\ge\dots\ge\cos\theta_p\), with \(p=\min(i,j)\). The angles \(\theta_\ell\) are the principal angles between the two column spaces. The paper's similarity measure is

$$\phi=\frac{\|U^{\top}V\|_F^2}{\min(i,j)}=\frac1p\sum_{\ell=1}^{p}\cos^2\theta_\ell\in[0,1].$$

\(\phi = 1\) iff one subspace contains the other, and \(0\) iff they are orthogonal. It equals \(1-d^2/p\), where \(d\) is the Grassmann projection-metric distance.

Tiny example

\(n=3\): \(U=[e_1\ e_2]\), \(V=[\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(e_1+e_2)\ \ e_3]\). Then \(U^{\top}V=\begin{pmatrix}1/\sqrt2&0\\1/\sqrt2&0\end{pmatrix}\), with singular values \(1, 0\), so \(\theta = 0^\circ, 90^\circ\). \(\phi=(1+0)/2=0.5\): the subspaces share exactly one direction, \((e_1+e_2)/\sqrt2\), and are orthogonal otherwise.

Intuition: random subspaces in high dimension barely overlap. \(\mathbb E\|U^{\top}V\|_F^2 = ij/n\), so for \(i=j=64\) in \(\mathbb R^{12288}\), \(\phi\approx 0.005\). That is why the “random Gaussian” panel of their Fig. 4 is black, and why \(\phi>0.5\) on the top direction is a strong signal.

Cool things

  • Rank 1 is enough on GPT-3. Adapting \(W_q, W_v\) with \(r=1\) gives 73.4 on WikiSQL and 91.3 on MNLI. With \(r=64\) the scores are 73.5 and 91.4. A rank-one update to a 12,288-dimensional map captures nearly the whole task.
  • The update amplifies directions \(W\) already has but doesn't emphasise. In layer 48, project \(W_q\) onto \(\Delta W_q\)'s top-4 singular subspaces: you get \(\|U_4^\top W_qV_4\|_F = 0.32\). Using \(W_q\)'s own top-4 directions instead gives 21.67, and random directions give 0.02. Meanwhile \(\|\Delta W_q\|_F = 6.91\), an amplification factor of about 21.5. So \(\Delta W\) is correlated with \(W\) (well above random) but avoids \(W\)'s top principal directions: it boosts features that pretraining learned but left quiet.
  • Spread the rank across matrices. At a fixed 18M-parameter budget, putting everything into \(W_q\) at \(r=8\) scores 70.4 on WikiSQL. Splitting it as \(W_q, W_v\) at \(r=4\) scores 73.7. Width across matrices beats depth in one.
  • The factors aren't identifiable. \(BA=(BC)(C^{-1}A)\) for any invertible \(r\times r\) matrix \(C\), a \(GL(r)\) symmetry. That's why the analysis compares subspaces (singular vectors and principal angles) rather than raw entries of \(A\).

If you remember one thing

Adapting a giant model moves each weight matrix along only a handful of directions, so the update can be written as \(BA\) with \(r\) as small as 1–4 with no loss in quality, and merged back into \(W_0\) at no inference cost.
