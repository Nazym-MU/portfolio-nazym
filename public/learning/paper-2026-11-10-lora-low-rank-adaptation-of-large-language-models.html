LoRA

Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models

Edward J. Hu, Yelong Shen, Phillip Wallis et al. · 2021 · arXiv:2106.09685

Fine-tuning a large language model for a new task normally rewrites every one of its weights, so each task costs a full copy of the model. LoRA freezes the original weights and learns each weight matrix's change as the product of two thin matrices. The paper shows that a change of rank 1 to 4 is often enough.

I · In 60 Seconds

Problem350 GB per task

A pretrained language model is a large set of weights learned once from a huge amount of text. Call all of them \(\Phi\). Fine-tuning adapts the model to one task, such as turning English questions into database queries, by training further on that task's examples. That changes every weight: \(\Phi\) becomes \(\Phi+\Delta\Phi\), where \(\Delta\Phi\) is the fine-tuning update, as large as the model itself.

For GPT-3 175B (OpenAI's model with 175 billion weights), each fine-tuned copy is a 350 GB file, and training it takes 1.2 TB of GPU memory. Ten tasks means ten such copies to store and serve.

The standard workaround is adapters: small extra layers inserted into the network and trained instead of the original weights. The per-task file is small, but the extra layers add computation at every step of generation, slowing it by 20–30% when serving one request at a time.

Idea\(\Delta W = BA\)

Keep the pretrained weights frozen and learn only the change, in a restricted form. Take one pretrained weight matrix \(W_0\) of size \(d\times k\) (in GPT-3, an attention matrix of size 12,288 × 12,288). Its change \(\Delta W\) is not learned entry by entry. LoRA writes it as \(\Delta W = BA\), a tall thin \(B\) (\(d\times r\)) times a wide thin \(A\) (\(r\times k\)), where the rank \(r\) is tiny, around 1 to 8. Only \(A\) and \(B\) are trained.

After training, add \(BA\) into \(W_0\) once. The result has the same shape as before, so the model runs exactly as fast as the original. A task is stored as just its small \(A, B\) pairs.

Result≈0.003% trained

On GPT-3 175B, LoRA trains 4.7 million numbers (about 0.003% of the model) and matches or beats full fine-tuning on three tasks: deciding whether one sentence implies another (MNLI, accuracy 91.7 vs 89.5), English to SQL (WikiSQL, 73.4 vs 73.8), and summarising chat conversations (SAMSum, word-overlap score 53.8 vs 52.0).

The per-task file shrinks from 350 GB to 35 MB, training memory from 1.2 TB to 350 GB, and generation is no slower.

II · The Linear Algebra

MachineryFrozen plus low rank

GPT-3 is a transformer: a stack of 96 layers, each holding one vector per word of the input. Inside each layer, attention lets words exchange information. It begins by multiplying every word's vector by learned square matrices: \(W_q\) produces “queries”, \(W_k\) “keys”, \(W_v\) “values”, and \(W_o\) maps the mixed result back. LoRA attaches to these matrices.

The objects, for one matrix

\(W_0\in\mathbb R^{d\times k}\)frozen

The pretrained matrix, sending a \(k\)-dimensional input \(x\) to a \(d\)-dimensional output. It is dense, essentially full-rank, and never updated. The paper adapts \(W_q\) and \(W_v\) in every layer, where \(d=k=12{,}288\) (GPT-3's vector width). Everything else stays frozen.

\(B\in\mathbb R^{d\times r}\)trainable

Trainable, starts at \(0\).

\(A\in\mathbb R^{r\times k}\)trainable

Trainable, starts with independent random Gaussian entries.

\(\Delta W = BA\)rank ≤ r

The learned change. Rank at most \(r\) is the only constraint; nothing like sparsity or orthogonality is imposed. Since \(B=0\) at the start, \(\Delta W=0\) and training begins exactly at the pretrained model.

The adapted layer computes its output \(h\) from input \(x\) as

$$h \;=\; W_0x + \frac{\alpha}{r}\,BAx, \qquad \#\text{trained} = r(d+k)\ \text{ instead of } dk.$$

Here \(\alpha\) is a fixed constant, not trained. Dividing by \(r\) keeps the update's size roughly constant as you change \(r\), so the learning rate needn't be re-tuned. For one GPT-3 matrix with \(r=1\), that is 24,576 trained numbers instead of 151 million.

Memory drops because the optimizer (Adam) keeps two running averages for every trained number. Frozen \(W_0\) needs neither those nor stored gradients.

At deployment you store \(W = W_0 + BA\). It has the same shape as \(W_0\), so a forward pass costs exactly what the base model's does. To switch tasks, subtract \(BA\) and add another task's \(B'A'\).

Why rank r should be enough: the update's singular values decay fast

Write any update by its singular value decomposition, with \(\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\dots\ge0\). By Eckart–Young, the best rank-\(r\) approximation keeps the first \(r\) terms, and the squared error is the tail:

$$\Delta W=\sum_{i}\sigma_i\,u_iv_i^{\top},\qquad \min_{\operatorname{rank}M\le r}\|\Delta W-M\|_F^2=\sum_{i>r}\sigma_i^2 .$$

\(W_0\) itself is full-rank. The paper's hypothesis is that the change a task needs has a negligible tail. Earlier work motivated this: pretrained models can be fine-tuned well while restricted to a random low-dimensional subspace.

LoRA never computes an SVD during training; it learns \(A\) and \(B\) directly. The SVD appears only in the paper's analysis (Section 7), which trains with \(r=8\) and \(r=64\) and compares the learned \(A\)'s. Their top singular direction is shared (similarity above 0.5, measured below). The remaining directions disagree across ranks and random seeds, so they look like noise.

How a factorised update learns, and why \(B\) starts at 0

Let \(L\) be the training loss and \(G=\partial L/\partial W\in\mathbb R^{d\times k}\) its gradient with respect to the whole matrix, the direction full fine-tuning would step in. With learning rate \(\eta\), the chain rule through \(W=W_0+BA\) gives

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial B}=G A^{\top},\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial A}=B^{\top}G,\qquad \delta(BA)\approx-\eta\big(G\,A^{\top}\!A+BB^{\top}G\big).$$

\(A^{\top}A\) (\(k\times k\)) and \(BB^{\top}\) (\(d\times d\)) are positive semidefinite with rank \(\le r\). So each LoRA step is the full step \(-\eta G\), filtered on the right and left by low-rank matrices: it only sees the part of \(G\) acting on the row space of \(A\) and the column space of \(B\).

Tiny example

\(d=k=2,\ r=1\): \(A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\end{pmatrix},\ B=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix},\ G=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\).

\(\partial L/\partial A = B^{\top}G = (0\ \ 0)\), so \(A\) does not move on step 1. \(\partial L/\partial B = GA^{\top} = \binom{1}{-2}\). With \(\eta = 0.1\), \(B=\binom{-0.1}{0.2}\), so \(\Delta W = BA=\begin{pmatrix}-0.1&-0.2\\0.2&0.4\end{pmatrix} = -\eta\,G A^{\top}A\).

Full fine-tuning would have stepped \(-\eta G=\begin{pmatrix}-0.1&0\\0&0.1\end{pmatrix}\), which is rank 2. LoRA keeps only \(G\)'s action on \(\operatorname{span}\{(1,2)\}\), scaled by \(\|A\|^2=5\).

So with \(B=0\), LoRA starts as gradient descent inside a random \(r\)-dimensional slice of input space, spanned by the Gaussian rows of \(A\). From the second step \(A\) moves too, so the slice itself is learned.

Measuring whether two learned subspaces agree (the paper's \(\phi\))

To compare two runs, take the top singular directions of each learned \(A\) and stack them as orthonormal columns: \(U\in\mathbb R^{n\times i}\) and \(V\in\mathbb R^{n\times j}\), with \(n\) the input dimension. The singular values of \(U^{\top}V\) are \(\cos\theta_1\ge\dots\ge\cos\theta_p\), \(p=\min(i,j)\), where the \(\theta_\ell\) are the principal angles between the two subspaces. The paper's similarity is their average squared cosine:

$$\phi=\frac{\|U^{\top}V\|_F^2}{\min(i,j)}=\frac1p\sum_{\ell=1}^{p}\cos^2\theta_\ell\in[0,1].$$

\(\phi = 1\) iff one subspace contains the other, and \(0\) iff they are orthogonal.

Tiny example

\(n=3\): \(U=[e_1\ e_2]\), \(V=[\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(e_1+e_2)\ \ e_3]\). Then \(U^{\top}V=\begin{pmatrix}1/\sqrt2&0\\1/\sqrt2&0\end{pmatrix}\), with singular values \(1, 0\), so \(\theta = 0^\circ, 90^\circ\). \(\phi=(1+0)/2=0.5\): the subspaces share exactly one direction, \((e_1+e_2)/\sqrt2\), and are orthogonal otherwise.

Random subspaces in high dimension barely overlap: \(\mathbb E\|U^{\top}V\|_F^2 = ij/n\), so for \(i=j=64\) in \(\mathbb R^{12288}\), \(\phi\approx 0.005\). That is why \(\phi>0.5\) on the top direction is a strong signal.

IV · Cool Things

FindingsFrom the paper's tables

Rank 1 is enough on GPT-3r = 1

Adapting \(W_q, W_v\) with \(r=1\) scores 73.4 on WikiSQL and 91.3 on MNLI. With \(r=64\) the scores are 73.5 and 91.4. A rank-one change to a 12,288-dimensional map captures nearly the whole task.

The update amplifies directions \(W\) has but doesn't emphasise× 21.5

Take layer 48 and a learned \(\Delta W_q\) of rank 4. Let \(U_4, V_4\) hold its top-4 left and right singular vectors. Then \(\|U_4^\top W_qV_4\|_F\) measures how strongly the pretrained \(W_q\) already acts along those directions.

It is 0.32. Along \(W_q\)'s own top-4 directions it would be 21.67, along random ones 0.02. Meanwhile \(\|\Delta W_q\|_F = 6.91\), so the update boosts those directions by about \(6.91/0.32\approx21.5\times\). \(\Delta W\) is related to \(W\) (well above random) but avoids its dominant directions: it amplifies features pretraining learned but left quiet.

Spread the rank across matrices18M budget

With 18M trained numbers to spend, putting all of them into \(W_q\) at \(r=8\) scores 70.4 on WikiSQL. Splitting them over \(W_q\) and \(W_v\) at \(r=4\) each scores 73.7. Adapting more matrices at lower rank beats one matrix at higher rank.

The factors aren't identifiable\(GL(r)\)

\(BA=(BC)(C^{-1}A)\) for any invertible \(r\times r\) matrix \(C\), so many \((A,B)\) pairs give the same update. That's why the analysis compares subspaces (singular vectors and principal angles) rather than raw entries of \(A\).

V · If You Remember One Thing

The one thing to remember

Adapting a giant model to a task changes each weight matrix along only a handful of directions. So the change can be learned as \(BA\), two thin matrices of rank 1 to 4, with no loss in quality, and then added into the frozen pretrained weights \(W_0\) at no inference cost.
