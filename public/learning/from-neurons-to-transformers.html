A short illustrated history of neural network architectures

From Neurons to Transformers

Eighty years of one argument — should a network pass the signal straight through, or loop it back?

feed-forward ↔ recurrent, and back again

Every architecture in this story exists because the previous one hit a wall. The perceptron could not draw a curve. The multilayer net could not see. The convolutional net could not remember. The recurrent net could not remember far enough. The gated recurrent net could not be parallelized. Each fix created the next problem, and the chain has not stopped.

Underneath the whole sequence runs one recurring tension. A feed-forward network sends input to output in a single pass and keeps no state, which makes it fast and trainable in parallel. A recurrent network threads a hidden state through a loop, which gives it memory at the price of going one step at a time. The field has crossed that line twice — and is, right now, crossing it a third time.

I · The Neuron & the Perceptron

RegimeFeed-forward

McCulloch & Pitts unit1943

A cartoon of a neuron: take a weighted sum of the inputs, and fire if the sum crosses a threshold. McCulloch and Pitts showed that networks of these binary threshold units could compute logical functions — AND, OR, NOT, and combinations of them. But there was no way for the network to learn; every weight had to be wired by hand.

Hebb's rule1949

Hebb supplied a biological learning principle: connections between neurons that activate together should strengthen — “cells that fire together wire together.” A principle, not yet an algorithm.

Rosenblatt's perceptron1958

Rosenblatt fused the two into something trainable: a single linear unit with an update rule that nudges the weights whenever the unit misclassifies an example. Crucially, the rule comes with a proof: if the data can be split by a straight line (it is linearly separable), the perceptron is guaranteed to find such a line in finitely many updates.

Problem solved

A machine that learns its own decision boundary from labeled examples, instead of having its weights wired by hand.

II · The XOR Wall & the First Winter

RegimeFeed-forward

Minsky & Papert, Perceptrons1969

Minsky and Papert proved that a single-layer perceptron cannot represent any function that is not linearly separable. The canonical counterexample is XOR — “exactly one of the two inputs is on” — whose four cases cannot be split by any single straight line. Plot them on a square: the two positive corners sit diagonally opposite each other, and no line puts them on one side alone.

Everyone understood, even then, that stacking layers would fix this in principle: a hidden layer can carve the plane into pieces a single line cannot. The trouble was that nobody knew how to train the hidden layers. Which weights inside the stack deserve the blame for an error at the output? Without an answer to that credit assignment question, deeper networks were unreachable. Funding and interest collapsed — the first AI winter.

Problem exposed

Linear models are fundamentally limited — and the obvious fix, hidden layers, was untrainable.

III · Backpropagation & the MLP

RegimeFeed-forward

The multilayer perceptron (MLP) stacks linear layers with nonlinear activation functions between them — the nonlinearity is what lets the stack bend its decision boundary into curves. The missing piece was how to assign credit to weights buried in the middle.

Reverse-mode differentiation1970 / 1974

The answer is reverse-mode automatic differentiation: nothing more exotic than the chain rule from calculus, applied backward through the computation graph. Run the network forward, then walk the error backward layer by layer, and each weight receives exactly its share of the blame — its gradient. Linnainmaa (1970) and Werbos (1974) had the mathematics worked out.

Backpropagation popularized1986

Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams named it backpropagation, showed it solving real tasks, and made the world notice. Gradient descent could now train arbitrarily deep stacks.

Universal approximation1989 / 1991

Cybenko and then Hornik proved that an MLP with a single hidden layer and enough units can approximate any continuous function to any precision. After that, raw expressiveness was no longer the worry. The worry became whether a network of practical size could learn the right function from data — which is a question about structure, not capacity.

Problem solved

Nonlinear decision boundaries — XOR included — trained end to end by gradient descent through hidden layers.

IV · The Convolutional Net

RegimeFeed-forward

An MLP treats its input as one flat vector, and on images that is ruinous. A 224×224 color image is roughly 150,000 numbers, so a single fully connected hidden layer already needs hundreds of millions of weights. Worse, the flat view has no idea that a cat shifted three pixels to the right is still a cat — every pixel position learns from scratch, independently.

Convolutional neural networks fix this with three structural ideas:

Local receptive fieldsidea 1

Each unit looks at only a small patch of the image, the way an edge is a local fact before a face is a global one.

Weight sharingidea 2

The same small filter slides across the entire image, so the parameter count scales with the filter size, not the image size. One edge detector serves every location. This is what cuts those hundreds of millions of weights down by orders of magnitude.

Poolingidea 3

Downsample between layers, keeping the strongest local responses, so small shifts and distortions stop mattering.

Together these give translation equivariance essentially for free: shift the input and the feature maps shift with it, rather than being scrambled. The lineage: Fukushima's Neocognitron (1980) had the structure; LeCun (1989, then LeNet-5 in 1998) added backprop training and read handwritten digits with it; AlexNet (Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton, 2012) scaled the recipe on GPUs, won ImageNet by a huge margin, and set off the modern deep learning boom.

Problem solved

Exploiting spatial structure, so vision becomes tractable and what is learned at one position generalizes to every other.

but note: a CNN is still feed-forward. fixed-size input, one pass, no memory — that's exactly the next wall.

V · Sequences Need Memory: The RNN

RegimeRecurrent — the loop enters

Text, speech, and time series have two properties that no feed-forward net can handle. They come in variable lengths — a sentence can be 5 words or 50 — and they are order-dependent: “dog bites man” is not “man bites dog.” An MLP or CNN takes a fixed-size input, makes one pass, and carries nothing from one input to the next.

The recurrent network1986–1990

Recurrent neural networks (Jordan, then Elman, 1990) add a hidden state that the network feeds back into itself at every timestep. The update is, roughly:

The recurrence

ht = f ( Wx xt + Wh ht−1 )

New memory = a function of the current input and the previous memory. The same weights W are reused at every step, so any length of sequence fits, and ht serves as a compressed summary of everything seen so far.

Training works by unrolling the loop: lay the network out as one long feed-forward chain, one copy per timestep (all sharing the same weights — the CNN trick again, but across time instead of space), and run ordinary backprop down the chain. This is called backpropagation through time.

This is the cleanest statement of the feed-forward versus recurrent divide. Feed-forward: input to output in one pass, no state, fully parallel. Recurrent: a loop threads a hidden state across timesteps, giving memory but forcing the steps into sequence.

Problem solved

Processing sequences of arbitrary length, with sensitivity to order.

VI · Vanishing Gradients: LSTM & GRU

RegimeRecurrent — now with gates

The vanishing gradient1991 / 1994

Backprop through time multiplies a long chain of Jacobians — the matrices of local derivatives — one per timestep. Multiply many numbers slightly below 1 and the product races to zero; slightly above 1 and it explodes. Hochreiter (1991) and Bengio et al. (1994) formalized this. The practical consequence: a vanilla RNN cannot connect information across long gaps — a pronoun to the noun forty words earlier — because the training signal linking them has vanished before it arrives.

LSTM1997

Hochreiter and Schmidhuber's Long Short-Term Memory adds a separate cell state: a conveyor belt running through time that is updated additively rather than by repeated matrix multiplication. Three learned gates — input, forget, output — decide what to write onto the belt, what to erase, and what to reveal to the rest of the network at each step.

The constant error carousel

Because the cell state's default update is addition, gradients flowing back along it are not repeatedly squashed by multiplication. The error can ride the belt across hundreds of steps intact. That protected additive path is the LSTM's whole trick — and the reason long-range dependencies survive training.

GRU2014

Cho et al.'s Gated Recurrent Unit is the lighter version: two gates instead of three, no separate cell state, comparable performance in practice.

Problem solved

Long-range dependencies — by protecting gradient flow with gates, not by removing the loop.

VII · The Fixed-Vector Bottleneck: Attention

RegimeRecurrent — with a lookback

Sequence-to-sequence2014

For translation, Sutskever, Vinyals, and Le chained two LSTMs: an encoder reads the whole input sentence and compresses it into one fixed-size vector; a decoder unfolds that vector into the output sentence. It worked — and had an obvious flaw. One vector simply cannot hold everything about a long sentence, and the decoder has nothing else to consult. Quality degraded as inputs grew.

Attention2015

Bahdanau, Cho, and Bengio let the decoder look back at all of the encoder's hidden states, at every output step. It takes a weighted average of them, with the weights computed on the fly — high weight on whatever input words matter for the word being produced right now. Instead of squeezing the sentence through one summary, the model learns where to look.

Problem solved

Information loss from a fixed-size summary — replaced by direct, learned access to every input position.

attention began as a patch bolted onto recurrent models. then someone asked: what if it's the only part we need?

VIII · Removing Recurrence: The Transformer

RegimeFeed-forward — the loop is cut

Two costs of recurrence remained. First, RNNs and LSTMs are inherently sequential: step t needs step t−1, so training cannot be parallelized across time — a serious tax on modern hardware built for parallel work. Second, even with gating, very long dependencies stay hard, because information must still survive passage through many sequential steps.

“Attention Is All You Need”2017

Vaswani et al. threw out the recurrence and kept only the attention. In self-attention, every position in the sequence attends directly to every other position. The path between any two tokens — however far apart — is one hop, constant length, rather than a relay proportional to their distance. Long-range dependencies become easy, and, crucially, the entire sequence is processed at once, in parallel. Since nothing in the architecture knows about order anymore, position is reintroduced explicitly, through positional encodings added to the inputs.

The rhyme to notice

The Transformer is feed-forward again — no loop, no state threaded through time. It solved the sequence problem the way the CNN solved the image problem: not with memory, but with the right structural prior (attention plus explicit position) baked into a single parallel pass.

Problem solved

Parallelizable training and constant-path long-range modeling — which is what unlocked scaling.

IX · What Came After

RegimeBoth, again

BERT & the GPT line2018–

The last several years of progress are, essentially, the Transformer scaled. BERT (2018) used a bidirectional encoder for understanding tasks; the GPT line (2018 onward) used a decoder for generation and showed that scaling parameters and data yields broad capability — a regularity precise enough to be written down as scaling laws.

Vision Transformers2021

Dosovitskiy et al. showed that, given enough data, attention beats convolution on images too. The architecture that replaced the RNN began displacing the CNN as well.

The quadratic wallopen

The Transformer carries its own unsolved problem, continuing the chain: self-attention compares every position with every other, so its cost grows as O(n²) in sequence length. Doubling the context quadruples the work. Very long contexts — books, codebases, hours of audio — get expensive fast.

S4 & Mamba2023–2024

That cost is driving work on efficient attention and on state space models such as S4 and Mamba (Gu and Dao). These bring back a recurrent, linear-time formulation — a state carried along the sequence, cost growing as O(n) — engineered so that training can still run in parallel and the old vanishing-gradient disease does not return. Memory without the quadratic bill.

The one thing to remember

The field abandoned recurrence for the perceptron's descendants, adopted it for sequences, abandoned it again for the Transformer's parallelism, and is now — with S4 and Mamba — revisiting the feed-forward versus recurrent tradeoff from the other direction. It was never a settled question. It is a dial, and each era turns it to pay for what the previous era could not afford.

every wall in this story was once somebody's solution.
