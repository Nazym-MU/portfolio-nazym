ConvNets for EEG

Deep Learning with Convolutional Neural Networks for EEG Decoding and Visualization

Robin Tibor Schirrmeister, Jost Tobias Springenberg, Lukas Dominique Josef Fiederer et al. · 2017 · arXiv:1703.05051

The paper trains neural networks directly on raw scalp voltage recordings to tell which limb a person is moving, shows they match the best hand-designed method, and then probes the trained networks to check they are listening to real brain rhythms.

I · In 60 Seconds

Problem288 examples per person

EEG (electroencephalography) records voltage over time from electrodes on the scalp. A brain–computer interface has to decode it: given a four-second recording, say whether the person moved (or imagined moving) their left hand, right hand or feet, or rested. Guessing gets 25%.

The standard decoder is a hand-built pipeline called filter bank common spatial patterns (FBCSP). It keeps one frequency band at a time, finds weighted sums of electrodes whose signal strength differs most between movements, and classifies on the logarithm of that strength. Every step encodes what neuroscientists already know about movement rhythms.

Convolutional networks, the models that took over image recognition by learning their own features from raw pixels, might do the same from raw voltages. But EEG is noisy, and there is very little of it: 288 training recordings per person in the public benchmark, against millions of images. It was unclear whether such networks could even match the pipeline.

Ideafilter, combine, square, log

Build networks whose first two layers mirror the pipeline: a learned filter along time, then a learned weighted sum across all electrodes. A two-layer “shallow” network follows these with squaring, averaging and a logarithm, so it is the pipeline with every step trainable. A five-layer “deep” network keeps going with generic layers and no built-in notion of signal strength.

To stretch the data, each recording is cut into 625 overlapping two-second windows that all share its label. And to see what was learned, the authors randomly nudge the strength of individual frequencies in the input and watch how the predictions move.

Result84.0% vs 82.1%

Averaged over 29 people in two datasets, FBCSP scores 82.1%, the deep network 84.0% and the shallow one 83.2%. With slow signals below 4 Hz removed (to keep eye movements out), FBCSP scores 81.9% and the shallow network 85.3%. The networks match the pipeline and are sometimes slightly, significantly better, never by more than 3.5 points.

That parity depends on three then-recent training techniques; without them the deep network is clearly worse than FBCSP. The probing shows the deep network learned, unprompted, to use the strength of the same three frequency bands the pipeline is built around.

II · The Linear Algebra

MachineryQuadratic forms of a covariance

Each electrode picks up a weighted sum of many electrical sources in the brain, and the same sources reach every electrode. So the channels are strongly correlated, nothing about the signal is local in space, and the natural operation across electrodes is a linear combination that undoes the mixing. In time, by contrast, the signal is made of rhythms at several scales. Every design choice below follows from that asymmetry.

The objects

\(X\in\mathbb R^{E\times T}\)one recording

Rows are the \(E\) electrodes, columns the \(T\) time samples. In the paper's own dataset \(E=44\) electrodes over the motor cortex, sampled 250 times a second; the network sees windows of about 2 seconds, \(T=534\).

\(k\in\mathbb R^{L}\)temporal kernel

A short list of weights slid along every row: \((x*k)(t)=\sum_{n}k_n\,x(t+n)\). As a matrix acting on a row it is banded and constant along diagonals (Toeplitz). The shallow network learns 40 kernels of length \(L=25\) (0.1 s); the deep one 25 kernels of length 10.

\(w\in\mathbb R^{E}\)spatial filter

One weight per electrode. It collapses the \(E\) rows into a single “virtual channel” \(s=w^{\top}X\in\mathbb R^{T}\). It spans all electrodes at once; it is not a small sliding patch.

\(\Sigma_c\in\mathbb R^{E\times E}\)symmetric PSD

The electrode-by-electrode covariance for movement class \(c\): the average of \(\tfrac1T XX^{\top}\) over that class's recordings (each row has mean zero). The variance, or power, of a virtual channel is then a quadratic form: \(\operatorname{Var}(w^{\top}X)=w^{\top}\Sigma_c\,w\).

The fact that does the work: one basis diagonalises two covariances

Movements show up in EEG mainly as changes in the power of rhythms. For instance, a 7–13 Hz rhythm over the brain's hand area weakens when that hand moves. So for two classes we want the \(w\) whose output power differs most between them. That is a ratio of quadratic forms (a Rayleigh quotient), and maximising it is a generalised eigenvalue problem:

$$\max_{w}\ \frac{w^{\top}\Sigma_1 w}{w^{\top}(\Sigma_1+\Sigma_2)\,w}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Sigma_1 w=\lambda\,(\Sigma_1+\Sigma_2)\,w .$$

Because both matrices are symmetric and the sum is positive definite, the \(E\) solutions can be stacked as the columns of one invertible matrix \(W\) with

$$W^{\top}(\Sigma_1+\Sigma_2)W=I,\qquad W^{\top}\Sigma_1W=\Lambda,\qquad W^{\top}\Sigma_2W=I-\Lambda,$$

where \(\Lambda\) is diagonal with entries \(\lambda_i\in[0,1]\). The virtual channels are uncorrelated in both classes at once. A filter with \(\lambda\) near 1 carries power almost only in class 1, one near 0 almost only in class 2, so the useful filters sit at both ends of the spectrum. \(W\) is not orthogonal; its columns are orthonormal in the inner product defined by \(\Sigma_1+\Sigma_2\).

This is “common spatial patterns” (CSP). The feature it passes on is the log-variance \(\log\big(\tfrac1T\,w^{\top}XX^{\top}w\big)\). FBCSP repeats it for a bank of frequency bands 6 to 8 Hz wide. The paper describes CSP in words and cites these equations rather than printing them.

Tiny example

Two electrodes: \(\Sigma_1=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},\ \Sigma_2=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}\). Each electrode alone has variance 2 in both classes, so neither tells them apart.

Here \(\Sigma_1+\Sigma_2=4I\), so the problem reduces to \(\Sigma_1w=4\lambda w\). \(\Sigma_1\) has eigenvalues 3 and 1 with eigenvectors \((1,1)/\sqrt2\) and \((1,-1)/\sqrt2\), giving \(\lambda=\tfrac34,\tfrac14\).

The sum channel \(w=(1,1)/\sqrt2\) has variance \(w^{\top}\Sigma_1w=3\) in class 1 and \(w^{\top}\Sigma_2w=1\) in class 2; the difference channel has the reverse. All the information was in the off-diagonal entries: how the electrodes move together.

The shallow network is that pipeline, made trainable

With \(k_f\) the temporal kernels and \(w_{g,f}\in\mathbb R^{E}\) the spatial weights that output channel \(g\) puts on kernel \(f\), the network computes, for each of its 40 channels \(g\) and each of 30 windows \(j\) of 75 samples (0.3 s),

$$s_g(t)=\sum_{f=1}^{40} w_{g,f}^{\top}\,(X*k_f)(t),\qquad \phi_{g,j}=\log\Big(\frac1{75}\sum_{t\in\text{window }j}s_g(t)^2\Big).$$

Since \(s_g\) has mean near zero, the mean of squares is its variance: \(\phi\) is the CSP log-variance. A final linear layer turns the \(40\times30\) features into four class scores. Two things differ from FBCSP. The kernels and spatial weights are found by gradient descent on the classification error, all at once, rather than by fixing bands and solving eigenproblems. And there are 30 windows per input, so the network can see power changing during a movement.

Why variance of a filtered signal means “band power”

Sliding a kernel commutes with shifting time, so pure oscillations are its eigenvectors: \(k*e^{i\omega t}=\hat k(\omega)\,e^{i\omega t}\), where \(\hat k(\omega)=\sum_n k_n e^{i\omega n}\) is the kernel's Fourier transform. (Exactly true for a signal that wraps around; true away from the edges otherwise.) In the Fourier basis the temporal layer is a diagonal matrix that multiplies frequency \(\omega\) by \(\hat k(\omega)\).

Let \(C(\omega)\in\mathbb C^{E\times E}\) be the covariance between electrodes at frequency \(\omega\) (Hermitian, PSD; these sum over \(\omega\) to \(\Sigma\)). Then one kernel and one spatial filter give

$$\operatorname{Var}\big(w^{\top}(X*k)\big)=\sum_{\omega}\ |\hat k(\omega)|^{2}\;w^{\top}C(\omega)\,w .$$

The kernel chooses which frequencies count, through \(|\hat k|^2\); the spatial filter chooses a direction in electrode space; the output is power in that band along that direction. The first interactive below is the \(w\) half of this formula and the second is the \(\hat k\) half.

Splitting the first layer: a factorisation, but not a low-rank one

There is no nonlinearity between the temporal and spatial layers, so together they are one linear map. Its effective filter for channel \(g\) is an \(E\times L\) matrix, a weight for every electrode at every time lag:

$$M_g=\sum_{f} w_{g,f}\,k_f^{\top}\in\mathbb R^{E\times L},\qquad s_g(t)=\big\langle M_g,\ X_{[t,\,t+L)}\big\rangle .$$

Each term \(w\,k^{\top}\) is rank one: a filter that separates into “which electrodes” times “which waveform”. The paper says the split “implicitly regularizes” the layer. A check the paper leaves out: with 40 kernels of length 25, the \(k_f\) generically span \(\mathbb R^{25}\), so every \(44\times25\) matrix is reachable. The split even has more parameters (\(40\cdot25+40\cdot40\cdot44=71{,}400\)) than learning the \(M_g\) directly (\(40\cdot44\cdot25=44{,}000\)). So the set of functions is unchanged; only the parametrisation, and hence the path gradient descent takes, differs. It helped most for the shallow network on unfiltered data, the one case where the gain was statistically significant.

Asking a trained network what it listens to

Take the Fourier transform of each row of \(X\) (a unitary change of basis), write each coefficient as amplitude times phase, add Gaussian noise \(\delta\) with mean 0 and variance 1 to the amplitudes, keep the phases, and transform back. Record the change \(\Delta f\) in the network's four class scores. Do this 400 times and correlate \(\delta\) at each (electrode, frequency) with \(\Delta f\) for each class.

To first order \(\Delta f\approx J\delta\), where \(J\) is the Jacobian of the scores with respect to the spectral amplitudes. Because the entries of \(\delta\) are independent, each correlation is proportional to one entry of \(J\). So the map is a sampled estimate of that Jacobian, and unlike a correlation between naturally occurring features and outputs, it is causal: the amplitude was changed by hand.

IV · Cool Things

FindingsFrom the paper's results

Three training tricks decide the outcomep < 0.001

The deep network matches FBCSP only with all three of: batch normalisation (rescaling each layer's outputs to mean 0 and variance 1 over a batch of examples), dropout (zeroing half of a layer's inputs at random during training), and exponential linear units (the activation \(f(x)=x\) for \(x>0\), \(e^{x}-1\) otherwise). Remove all three and it is significantly worse than the hand-built pipeline. Swapping only the activation for the common \(\max(x,0)\) also hurt significantly, and the authors say they cannot explain why.

Thirty-one layers lose to five, and five tie with two80.6% vs 84.0%

A 31-layer residual network, the architecture then winning image benchmarks, scored 80.6%: below the 5-layer network (84.0%) and below FBCSP (82.1%). The 2-layer shallow network was as good as the deep one. The authors' reading: either a few hundred recordings are too few, or the informative part of EEG simply has little of the part-within-part hierarchy that depth exploits in images.

625 training examples from one recordingp < 10⁻⁵

Sliding a two-second window one sample at a time gives 625 crops per recording; at test time the 625 predictions are averaged. Neighbouring crops overlap almost entirely, and because convolutions commute with shifts their intermediate outputs are shared, so all crops are computed in a single pass rather than 625. For the deep network on data above 4 Hz this raised accuracy for almost every person. The authors' explanation: the same rhythm appears at every shift, so the network cannot memorise where the peaks fall and is pushed toward power.

The network rediscovered the physiology, with a caveat3 peaks

The perturbation maps for the deep network peak in three bands: 7–13 Hz, 13–31 Hz and 71–91 Hz, the same bands where plain CSP decodes best. Over the scalp, the sensitive electrodes for each hand sit above the opposite side's motor area, and for the feet at the top of the head, as textbooks predict. The perturbed inputs were still classified at over 99.5% of the original accuracy, so the probe stayed on realistic data.

The caveat is linear algebra: the map shows where the network is sensitive, not where the brain signal is. A filter can weight an electrode heavily just to subtract noise it shares with another, exactly as the difference channel \((1,-1)\) does in the tiny example.

V · If You Remember One Thing

The one thing to remember

Decoding movement from EEG comes down to the log of a quadratic form \(w^{\top}\Sigma\,w\) of a band-limited covariance matrix; a network that filters in time, combines electrodes, squares, averages and takes a log computes exactly that with every piece learned, and trained on raw recordings it matches the hand-built eigenvector method.
