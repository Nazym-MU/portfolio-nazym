DeepLabCut

Markerless tracking of user-defined features with deep learning

Alexander Mathis, Pranav Mamidanna, Taiga Abe et al. · 2018 · arXiv:1804.03142

Neuroscientists film animals and need the position of specific body parts, such as a mouse's snout or the joints of its paw, in every frame. This paper takes an image network already trained on everyday photos, retrains it on about 200 hand-labelled frames, and gets a tracker whose accuracy is close to that of a human labeller.

I · In 60 Seconds

Problemmarkers are intrusive

To study movement, researchers record video and then need coordinates: where the wrist is in frame 1, frame 2, and so on, for tens of thousands of frames. The classic approach glues reflective markers onto the body and lets a camera system find them. On a mouse or a fruit fly the markers are physically in the way. You also have to decide in advance which points to track. Labelling every frame by hand is accurate but far too slow.

Ideatransfer learning

Start from a deep network (ResNet-50) that has already been trained to recognise objects in ImageNet, a collection of about a million labelled photos. Its early layers already respond to edges, textures and shapes. Replace its final classification layer with a small new output layer that produces, for each body part the user chose, a coarse map of how likely that part is to be at each location. A second output is a small correction vector that pinpoints the location within a cell of that map. Then train the whole network on a few hundred frames that a person has labelled. The method comes from a human pose estimator called DeeperCut, which the authors repurposed for animals.

Result≈200 frames

The same pipeline tracks a mouse's snout and ears as it follows an odour trail, a fruit fly's body parts while it lays eggs, and individual finger joints of a mouse reaching for food. With only about 200 labelled frames, its errors on frames it never saw are comparable to the disagreement between human labellers. Every prediction also comes with a confidence score, so frames where a part is hidden can be flagged.

II · The Linear Algebra

MachineryShift-commuting operators

A video frame is a vector: an image of height \(H\), width \(W\), and 3 colour channels is a point of \(\mathbb R^{H\cdot W\cdot 3}\). The user picks \(J\) body parts (say \(J=4\): wrist and three fingertips). The network maps a frame to two outputs on a coarse grid that is 8 times smaller in each direction, \(h=H/8,\ w=W/8\). Each grid cell corresponds to an 8×8 patch of pixels.

Score maps \(S_j\in[0,1]^{h\times w}\)one per part

\(S_j(c)\) is the predicted probability that part \(j\) lies within a fixed radius of the centre of cell \(c\). During training, the target map is 1 on cells near the labelled point and 0 elsewhere, and each cell is scored with a separate logistic (cross-entropy) loss.

Offset fields \(O_j\in\mathbb R^{h\times w\times 2}\)sub-cell correction

\(O_j(c)\) is a 2-vector: the predicted displacement from the centre of cell \(c\) to the true point. It is trained only on cells near the label, with a Huber loss (squared error for small mistakes, absolute error for large ones, so a few bad labels can't dominate).

Write \(\mu(c)\) for the pixel coordinates of cell \(c\)'s centre. The prediction for part \(j\) and its confidence are

$$c^\star=\arg\max_{c}\,S_j(c),\qquad \hat p_j=\mu(c^\star)+O_j(c^\star),\qquad \text{confidence}_j=S_j(c^\star).$$

Everything between the frame and these maps is a composition of linear operators (convolutions) and pointwise nonlinearities. The structure of those linear operators is why a score map is the right output.

A convolution is a sparse, weight-shared matrix

A convolutional layer takes \(C_{\text{in}}\) feature maps of size \(h\times w\) to \(C_{\text{out}}\) maps. It is linear, so it is a matrix \(K\) of size \((h w C_{\text{out}})\times(h w C_{\text{in}})\). It has \(C_{\text{out}}\times C_{\text{in}}\) blocks. Each block is doubly block Toeplitz: constant along diagonals, because the same 3×3 filter is applied at every position. Each row has only \(9C_{\text{in}}\) nonzeros, so \(K\) is extremely sparse, and it has only \(9C_{\text{in}}C_{\text{out}}\) free parameters however large the image.

The fact that does the work: convolutions commute with shifts

Let \(T\) be the operator that shifts an image by one pixel (wrapping around at the border, to keep it exact). With wraparound, each block of \(K\) is circulant, and every circulant matrix is diagonalised by the discrete Fourier transform \(F\):

$$K_{\text{block}}=F^{*}\Lambda F,\qquad T=F^{*}DF\quad(\Lambda, D\ \text{diagonal})\ \Longrightarrow\ KT=TK.$$

Pointwise nonlinearities such as ReLU also commute with \(T\). So a stack of stride-1 convolutions is shift-equivariant: if the mouse moves 5 pixels right, every feature map, and the final score map, moves 5 pixels right. A detector learned for a paw in one corner works everywhere, which is why a few hundred labelled frames are enough to cover the whole field of view.

The catch is that the network downsamples. A convolution with stride \(s\) (evaluated only at every \(s\)-th position) commutes only with shifts by multiples of \(s\). The overall stride here is 8, so the score map alone can only say which 8×8 cell the part is in. The offset field \(O_j\) restores the lost sub-cell position.

Tiny example: the offset's job

Stride 8, so cell centres sit at pixel coordinates \(4, 12, 20, 28, 36, 44,\dots\). The true wrist is at \(p=(39,\,23)\). The nearest centre is \(\mu(c^\star)=(36,\,20)\).

Argmax alone: error \(\|(3,3)\|=\sqrt{18}\approx 4.24\) px. The offset target is \(p-\mu(c^\star)=(3,3)\); a perfect offset head gives error 0. The worst case without offsets is a point on a cell corner, \(\|(4,4)\|=4\sqrt2\approx5.66\) px. Tracking fingertips needs better than that.

Going back up: the transposed convolution is literally \(K^{\top}\)

ResNet-50 normally shrinks the image by 32 in each direction. The tracker keeps more resolution inside the network and then uses a transposed convolution (often called “deconvolution”) to upsample to stride 8. If \(K\) is a strided convolution, the upsampling layer applies \(K^{\top}\) with learned weights. It is not an inverse: \(K\) is wide, so \(K^{\top}K\) is singular. It spreads each coarse value back over the pixels it came from.

Tiny example

1-D input \(x\in\mathbb R^4\), filter \((1,2)\), stride 2. The outputs are \(y_0=x_0+2x_1\) and \(y_1=x_2+2x_3\):

$$K=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0&0\\0&0&1&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad K^{\top}\!\begin{pmatrix}y_0\\y_1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}y_0\\2y_0\\y_1\\2y_1\end{pmatrix},\qquad KK^{\top}=5I.$$

Here the windows don't overlap, so \(K\)'s rows are orthogonal. Now take filter \((1,1,1)\) with stride 2 on \(\mathbb R^7\): rows cover pixels \(\{0,1,2\},\{2,3,4\},\{4,5,6\}\), and \(K^{\top}\mathbf 1=(1,1,2,1,2,1,1)\). Every other pixel gets twice the coverage. That alternating pattern is the origin of the well-known “checkerboard” artefacts in upsampled maps. In the interior, coverage is even exactly when the stride divides the filter length.

\(K^{\top}\) is also exactly what backpropagation applies when it sends gradients through \(K\) to the layer's input. So the “deconvolution” head is the gradient pass of a convolution, run forwards with its own learned filter.

Transfer learning: where the 200 frames go

Let \(\theta_0\) be ResNet-50's ImageNet weights. Training starts at \(\theta_0\), with a freshly initialised output head, and updates all weights on the labelled frames. Because the starting features already span edges, textures and parts, the task mostly needs to learn which combination of existing channels lights up at a wrist. That is a small change from \(\theta_0\), which is why roughly 200 examples suffice where training from random weights would need far more.

IV · Cool Things

Human pose to mouse fingerstransfer

The architecture was built to find elbows and knees of people in photos. Features learned from everyday objects, followed by an architecture built for human bodies, end up tracking fly legs and mouse knuckles. The user decides what counts as a “feature”: anything a person can click on consistently can be tracked.

Maps, not coordinatesequivariance

A network could regress the \((x,y)\) numbers directly, but then the last layers would have to turn a spatial pattern into two numbers, discarding the shift structure the convolutions provide for free. A score map instead inherits the shift-commuting structure of the convolutions, so “where” becomes “which cell lights up”. The same idea runs through segmentation networks and keypoint detectors in graphics.

Confidence for free\(S_j(c^\star)\)

The peak height of the score map is a usable confidence: when a finger goes behind the food pellet, the peak drops. Downstream analysis can drop or interpolate those frames instead of trusting a confident-looking but wrong coordinate.

Upsampling is a transpose\(K^{\top}\)

The layer that brings the maps back up to useful resolution is the adjoint of a strided convolution, the same operator backprop already uses. Its overlap pattern \(K^{\top}\mathbf 1\) explains checkerboard artefacts before you train anything.

V · If You Remember One Thing

The one thing to remember

Because convolutions commute with shifts, a pretrained image network retrained on about 200 labelled frames outputs a probability map that moves with the animal. Its peak, plus a small learned offset to undo the 8-pixel grid, tracks any body part about as accurately as a person can label it.
