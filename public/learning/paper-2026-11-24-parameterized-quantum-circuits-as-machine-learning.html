Circuits That Learn

Parameterized Quantum Circuits as Machine Learning Models

Marcello Benedetti, Erika Lloyd, Stefan Sack et al. · 2019 · cited 1,529 times · arXiv:1906.07682

A review of how a small, noisy quantum computer can be used as a trainable model: a quantum circuit whose gates have adjustable rotation angles produces the predictions, and an ordinary computer adjusts those angles, step by step, until the predictions fit the data.

I · In 60 Seconds

Problemtens of noisy qubits

A quantum computer works on qubits, two-level quantum systems, by applying a sequence of elementary operations called gates. The machines that exist have a few tens of qubits and can run only a short sequence of gates before noise swamps the answer. The famous algorithms, such as Shor's for factoring integers, need long sequences on far larger, error-corrected machines, so they are out of reach.

The question is what a short, noisy circuit is good for today.

Ideaa hybrid loop

Treat the circuit as a machine learning model. Some of its gates are rotations by an adjustable angle; those angles are the model's parameters, exactly like the weights of a neural network. The quantum computer does one thing: prepare the state the circuit prescribes and measure it. An ordinary (classical) computer does everything else: it turns the measurement results into a prediction, scores the prediction against the training data, and proposes better angles.

The two run in a closed loop. Such a circuit is called a parameterized quantum circuit.

Result15 hardware demos

This is a review, so the result is a map. Every model splits into the same parts: a circuit that loads a data point, a circuit with trainable angles, a measurement, and classical post-processing. One recipe covers classification, regression, generating new samples, and tasks whose data are themselves quantum states.

The paper tabulates 15 experiments on real hardware. All but one use between 1 and 6 qubits (the exception, a clustering experiment, uses 19). The authors say plainly that every supervised-learning experiment so far used scaled-down, often trivial, datasets. Whether these models beat classical ones on a real task is still open.

II · The Linear Algebra

MachineryUnitaries with two eigenvalues

The state of \(n\) qubits is a unit vector in \(\mathbb C^{2^n}\), with one coordinate per bit string of length \(n\). The joint state of independent qubits is the tensor (Kronecker) product of their individual states, which is why each added qubit doubles the dimension.

The objects

\(|0\rangle\in\mathbb C^{2^n}\)start

The starting state: the first standard basis vector \((1,0,\dots,0)\), meaning every qubit reads 0. \(\langle 0|\) is its conjugate transpose, a row vector.

\(U_{\phi(x)}\), unitary \(2^n\times 2^n\)encoder

The encoder circuit. \(x\) is one data vector and \(\phi\) is a classical pre-processing function that turns \(x\) into gate angles. This is the only place the data enters.

\(U_\theta\), unitary \(2^n\times 2^n\)trainable

The variational circuit: a product of \(J\) gates, \(U_\theta=U_J\cdots U_1\), where gate \(j\) depends on one real angle \(\theta_j\). The vector \(\theta\in\mathbb R^J\) is what training adjusts. \(J\) grows polynomially in \(n\), while the group of all unitaries on \(\mathbb C^{2^n}\) has \(4^n\) real dimensions, so the circuit reaches only a thin slice of it. That restriction is the model's structure.

\(M\), Hermitian \(2^n\times 2^n\)observable

The quantity measured at the end. Its spectral decomposition is \(M=\sum_i\lambda_iP_i\), with real eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\) and \(P_i\) the orthogonal projector onto the \(i\)-th eigenspace.

Measurement is a trace against a rank-one projector

Write \(U\) for the whole circuit and \(\rho=U|0\rangle\langle 0|U^\dagger\) for the projector onto the final state (\(\dagger\) is the conjugate transpose). \(\rho\) is positive semidefinite with rank one and trace one. The Born rule says one run of the circuit returns a single eigenvalue of \(M\), at random, with probability \(p(\lambda_i)=\operatorname{tr}(P_i\rho)\). Averaging gives the paper's first equation, the expectation value:

$$\langle M\rangle=\sum_i\lambda_i\,p(\lambda_i)=\operatorname{tr}(M\rho)=\langle 0|U^\dagger MU|0\rangle .$$

This number is the model's raw output. It is never read off directly; it is estimated by running the circuit many times (each run is a shot) and averaging.

The model is a linear function of a matrix

Split the circuit as \(U=U_\theta U_{\phi(x)}\) and let \(\rho(x)=U_{\phi(x)}|0\rangle\langle0|U_{\phi(x)}^\dagger\) be the projector onto the encoded data point. Cycling the trace,

$$\langle M\rangle_{x,\theta}=\operatorname{tr}\!\big(W_\theta\,\rho(x)\big),\qquad W_\theta=U_\theta^\dagger MU_\theta .$$

So the output is the trace inner product of a data-dependent matrix \(\rho(x)\) with a weight matrix \(W_\theta\): a linear classifier in the \(4^n\)-dimensional real space of Hermitian matrices. \(W_\theta\) is unitarily similar to \(M\), so its eigenvalues never change. Training only rotates its eigenvectors.

The encoder is a feature map, and it defines a kernel

A feature map sends each data point to a vector in a bigger space where the problem may be easier; a kernel is the similarity score between two mapped points. Here the map is \(x\mapsto U_{\phi(x)}|0\rangle\), and the kernel is the squared overlap of two encoded states:

$$k(x,x')=\big|\langle0|U_{\phi(x')}^\dagger U_{\phi(x)}|0\rangle\big|^2=\operatorname{tr}\!\big(\rho(x)\rho(x')\big).$$

The second form shows that the matrix of all pairwise kernel values is a Gram matrix (of the \(\rho\)'s under the trace inner product), hence symmetric positive semidefinite. That is exactly what classical kernel methods, such as the support vector machine, need.

The simplest encoder, qubit encoding, uses one qubit per input component \(x_i\in[0,1]\) and rotates it by an angle set by \(x_i\):

$$x\;\mapsto\;\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\begin{pmatrix}\cos(x_i\pi/2)\\ \sin(x_i\pi/2)\end{pmatrix},\qquad k(x,x')=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\cos^2\!\Big(\frac{\pi}{2}(x_i-x'_i)\Big).$$

The kernel formula follows because inner products multiply across a tensor product, and \(\cos a\cos b+\sin a\sin b=\cos(a-b)\).

Tiny example

One qubit, \(x=0\) and \(x'=0.5\). The states are \((1,0)\) and \((\cos45^\circ,\sin45^\circ)=(0.707,\,0.707)\). Their inner product is \(0.707\), so \(k=0.5\).

Two qubits with \(x=(0,0)\), \(x'=(0.5,0.5)\): the states live in \(\mathbb C^4\), \((1,0,0,0)\) and \((0.5,0.5,0.5,0.5)\). Inner product \(0.5=0.707^2\), so \(k=0.25\). The overlaps multiplied.

The opposite extreme is amplitude encoding: write a unit data vector with \(2^n\) entries directly as the state of \(n\) qubits. Memory use is exponentially small, but for generic data the circuit that prepares the state is expected to need exponentially many gates.

The spectral fact: each trainable gate has only two eigenvalues

The Pauli matrices are the \(2\times2\) matrices

$$X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.$$

A Pauli string \(P\) is a tensor product of \(n\) factors, each one of \(I, X, Y, Z\). It is both Hermitian and unitary, so \(P^2=I\) and its eigenvalues are \(\pm1\). In the most common parameterization, trainable gate \(j\) is the exponential of a Pauli string \(P_j\):

$$U_j=\exp\!\Big(-\frac{i}{2}\theta_jP_j\Big)=\cos\frac{\theta_j}{2}\,I-i\sin\frac{\theta_j}{2}\,P_j .$$

The closed form is the power series collapsing because \(P_j^2=I\). The gate's eigenvalues are just \(e^{-i\theta_j/2}\) and \(e^{+i\theta_j/2}\).

Now see how one gate \(U=cI-isP\) (with \(c=\cos\frac\theta2\), \(s=\sin\frac\theta2\)) transforms any Hermitian \(M\). Multiplying out and using the double-angle identities:

$$U^\dagger MU=\frac{M+PMP}{2}+\cos\theta\,\frac{M-PMP}{2}+\sin\theta\,\frac{i}{2}\,(PM-MP).$$

Only \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\) appear, because the two eigenvalues' phases differ by exactly \(\theta\). So, holding every other angle fixed, the expectation value as a function of one angle is a single sinusoid, for constants \(a,b,c\) that depend on the rest of the circuit:

$$\langle M\rangle(\theta_j)=a+b\cos\theta_j+c\sin\theta_j .$$

A sinusoid's derivative is the same sinusoid shifted by a quarter period. That gives the parameter shift rule, the paper's Eq. (5), where \(e_j\) is the \(j\)-th standard basis vector of \(\mathbb R^J\):

$$\frac{\partial\langle M\rangle_\theta}{\partial\theta_j}=\frac{\langle M\rangle_{\theta+\frac{\pi}{2}e_j}-\langle M\rangle_{\theta-\frac{\pi}{2}e_j}}{2}.$$

It is exact, with no small step size, and uses the same circuit run at two shifted angles.

Tiny example

One qubit, gate \(e^{-i\theta X/2}\), measure \(Z\). The state is \((\cos\frac\theta2,\,-i\sin\frac\theta2)\), so \(\langle Z\rangle=\cos^2\frac\theta2-\sin^2\frac\theta2=\cos\theta\). In the formula above, \(XZX=-Z\) kills the constant term and leaves \(U^\dagger ZU=\cos\theta\,Z+\sin\theta\,Y\).

At \(\theta=\pi/3\): outcome probabilities \(0.75\) and \(0.25\), \(\langle Z\rangle=0.5\), true derivative \(-\sin\frac\pi3=-0.866\).

Shift rule: \(\tfrac12\big[\cos\tfrac{5\pi}{6}-\cos(-\tfrac{\pi}{6})\big]=\tfrac12[-0.866-0.866]=-0.866\). Exact.

Why this matters: a quantum state cannot be inspected mid-circuit without destroying it, so there is no stored forward pass to run backpropagation on. The alternative, a finite difference \([L(\theta+\Delta e_j)-L(\theta-\Delta e_j)]/2\Delta\) with small \(\Delta\), is biased and divides noisy measurements by a small number. The shift rule gives an unbiased gradient from \(2J\) runs of circuits the hardware can already execute.

III · Try It: A Gradient From Two Runs

InteractiveShift versus finite difference

This is the one-qubit example: gate \(e^{-i\theta X/2}\), measure \(Z\), so the curve is \(\langle Z\rangle=\cos\theta\). Each shot returns \(+1\) or \(-1\); the two filled dots are averages over the chosen number of shots at \(\theta+s\) and \(\theta-s\). Open circles are the exact values. Two formulas turn the dots into a slope: the finite difference divides their gap by \(2s\); the shift formula divides by \(2\sin s\), which is exact for a sinusoid at any shift \(s\) and becomes the paper's rule at \(s=\pi/2\).

Try: set the shift to its smallest value and press measure again a few times. Then slide the shift to \(\pi/2\) and do the same. Notice: at a small shift the dashed estimated slope swings wildly, because shot noise is divided by a tiny number (spread about 1.2 at 100 shots, larger than the slope itself). At \(\pi/2\) the spread falls to about 0.04. There the finite-difference formula is also systematically wrong, reading \(2/\pi\approx0.64\) of the true slope, while the shift formula is right on average.

A second dial: repeating the encoding sharpens the kernel

Take a one-number input \(x\in[0,1]\) and encode it into \(m\) qubits, each by the same rotation. Because overlaps multiply across tensor products, the kernel is \(k(x,x')=\cos^{2m}\!\big(\tfrac\pi2(x-x')\big)\). The plot shows \(k\) against the gap \(x-x'\).

Try: raise \(m\) from 1 to 16. Notice: the bump narrows like \(1/\sqrt m\) and settles onto the dotted curve \(e^{-m\pi^2(x-x')^2/4}\), a Gaussian kernel. Copies of the same qubit make nearby inputs look more different: this is the redundancy the paper says lets a circuit fit more non-linear functions.

IV · Cool Things

FindingsFrom the review

A linear circuit, a non-linear modelno activations

Every gate is unitary, hence linear, so there is nothing like a neural network's activation function inside the circuit. The non-linearity comes from the two ends: the data enters through \(\cos\) and \(\sin\) of rotation angles, and the output is quadratic in the state's entries. Repeating the encoding on more qubits raises the degree, as in the second dial above.

Gradients cannot explode, but they vanishbarren plateaus

A unitary has all singular values equal to 1, so a signal passed through many layers never blows up. Classical recurrent networks are sometimes constrained to be unitary to get this property; circuits have it for free.

The opposite failure is severe. For circuits with randomly chosen angles and reasonable depth, the gradient's variance shrinks exponentially with the number of qubits (McClean et al.). The cause is concentration of measure: a smooth function on the unit sphere of \(\mathbb C^{2^n}\) is nearly constant almost everywhere. The landscape is an exponentially large flat plateau, and the optimizer cannot tell the gradient from shot noise.

A sampler you cannot evaluateBorn machine

Drop the encoder and measure every qubit. Bit string \(v\) comes out with probability \(q_\theta(v)=|\langle v|U_\theta|0\rangle|^2\), the squared magnitude of one entry of a unit vector, so the probabilities sum to 1 with no normalizing constant to compute. This generative model is the quantum circuit Born machine.

Drawing a sample is one run of the circuit. Computing \(q_\theta(v)\) for a given \(v\) is believed intractable beyond small circuits. The parameter shift rule still gives gradients, even though the samples are discrete, which is exactly where classical generative models of this kind struggle.

A kernel built to be hard classically2 qubits so far

Havlíček et al. use the encoder \(\exp\!\big(i\sum_{j,k}\phi_{j,k}(x)Z_jZ_k\big)H^{\otimes n}\), where \(Z_j\) is Pauli \(Z\) on qubit \(j\) and \(H\) is the Hadamard gate, which sends \(|0\rangle\) to an equal mix of 0 and 1. The first factor spreads the state evenly over all \(2^n\) coordinates; the second is a diagonal unitary that multiplies each coordinate by a data-dependent phase. They conjecture that two such layers give a kernel no classical computer can estimate efficiently.

The quantum device then only estimates kernel values, and a classical support vector machine does the learning. It was demonstrated on 2 superconducting qubits. Whether a kernel being hard to compute makes it useful is, the authors stress, an open empirical question.

V · If You Remember One Thing

The one thing to remember

A trainable quantum gate is \(e^{-i\theta P/2}\) with \(P^2=I\), so it has just two eigenvalues, the measured output is a plain sinusoid in each angle, and its exact derivative is half the difference of two runs of the same circuit shifted by \(\pm\pi/2\). That is what lets a classical optimizer train a quantum circuit it can never look inside.
